Clients ask me what an LLC costs, and the honest answer is that the filing fee is the least interesting number on the page. Kentucky will form your LLC for $40. Massachusetts wants $500. But the fee you pay once matters far less than the fee you pay every year, forever, which ranges from nothing at all to California's $800 franchise tax. So this table shows all three numbers for every state: the filing fee, the ongoing fee, and the total you will actually send the state across your first five years.

## The 5-year column is the one to compare

Sorted by five-year total, the spread is stark: Missouri, New Mexico, Mississippi, and Arizona keep an LLC alive for about $50 total, while California costs $4,110, Massachusetts $3,000, Nevada $2,175, Delaware $2,110, and Tennessee $1,800 at the minimum. That is an 80x difference for the identical legal wrapper.

Two things the totals deliberately exclude, because they depend on choices you make: registered agent service (free if you list yourself, which most single-member LLCs safely can, or $100 to $150 a year hired) and income taxes, which follow where you earn, not where you file. Which leads to the mistake I see most.

## The out-of-state LLC trap

At least once a quarter someone tells me they are forming in Wyoming or Delaware "for the fees" or "for privacy" while living and working somewhere else. Here is what actually happens: your home state considers you to be doing business where you physically operate, so you must register the out-of-state LLC there as a foreign LLC. Foreign registration usually costs as much as forming domestically, and then you pay both states' annual fees for the life of the company. A Californian with a Wyoming LLC pays Wyoming $60 a year for nothing, because California still collects its $800.

The exceptions are narrow: businesses genuinely operating in multiple states, real estate held in the property's state, and startups raising institutional money (investors do prefer Delaware, but they prefer Delaware corporations, not LLCs). If none of those describe you, form at home. ## Cheap states that get expensive, and expensive states that do not

The table's "watch out for" column flags the traps, but a few deserve a sentence:

- **New York looks like $218 over five years and is not.** The publication requirement (running formation notices in two newspapers for six weeks) adds roughly $300 upstate and up to $1,600 in New York City. It is a one-time cost, but it surprises nearly everyone.
- **Texas charges no annual fee but demands annual paperwork.** The franchise tax report and Public Information Report are due every May even when you owe zero tax. Miss them and the state forfeits your LLC's charter.
- **Nevada's $425 is not the whole story going in, but it is honest going forward.** The mandatory business license and annual list are baked into the numbers above, which is exactly why Nevada quietly costs more than Delaware over five years despite its no-income-tax reputation.
- **Pennsylvania is the sleeper deal among big states.** A $125 filing and $7 a year make it cheaper to maintain than any of its neighbors.

## Where the LLC fee fits in your real startup budget

State fees are usually under 10% of what it costs to get a business running; the rest is equipment, inventory, insurance, and working capital. Once the LLC exists, the decisions that actually move your tax bill are [how you pay yourself](/how-to-pay-yourself-from-an-llc/) and whether an S corp election makes sense, which our [LLC versus S corp breakdown](/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better/) walks through with real numbers.

Fees in this table change when legislatures meet, usually in small increments and usually upward. I verify the full table against Secretary of State fee schedules when states publish changes, and the note at the top of the table carries the as-of date.
