If you're a [small business owner](/best-retirement-plan-for-small-business-owner-solo-401k-vs-sep-ira/) or federal contractor, this week matters. The SBA's proposed overhaul of small business size standards, covering 338 industry groups, has a public comment deadline of November 20, 2026. That's this Thursday. After that, the window closes, and the agency moves toward finalizing rules that could reshape who qualifies for SBA loans and federal contracting set-asides for years to come.

The proposal, published in the Federal Register on August 20, 2026, would revise the methodology the SBA uses to calculate size thresholds across hundreds of industries. The SBA initially extended the comment period by 60 days, pushing the deadline to November 20, after congressional criticism that some proposed thresholds represented increases of over 1,000% and could effectively crowd smaller firms out of programs designed to protect them. As of mid-October, the agency had already received roughly 1,200 comments, and the debate inside those submissions is sharp.

## What the SBA Is Actually Proposing

The core change is methodological. The SBA sets size standards using revenue caps or employee counts, depending on the industry. [Right now](/how-to-use-bonus-depreciation-right-now-before-year-end/) those thresholds vary considerably across sectors, and critics have argued for years that the methodology is outdated. The new proposed approach would recalibrate thresholds based on updated industry data, with the stated goal of making the standards more accurately reflect current market conditions.

The headline number sounds significant: between 110,000 and 114,500 additional businesses could qualify as small under the new thresholds, a roughly 1.8% to 2% increase in the total small business population. For those firms, that would mean new access to SBA 7(a) and 504 loan programs, plus eligibility for federal contracts set aside for [small businesses](/the-obbba-childcare-credit-most-small-businesses-are-ignoring/).

But the SBA's own Office of Advocacy revised that number down hard. According to the Office of Advocacy's analysis, only approximately 4,000 to 6,000 businesses that currently hold small contractor status would actually gain meaningful small-business classification under the new rule. That's a long way from the 37,002 firms SBA initially cited as likely beneficiaries. The gap between those two figures is worth paying attention to. It tells you something about how complicated the downstream effects of these changes actually are.

## Why Some Existing Small Businesses Are Worried

The concern coming from many current small contractors is straightforward: if you raise the size thresholds dramatically, you let much larger companies into the pool. And a larger company competing for set-aside contracts isn't the same as a small firm competing. The whole point of set-asides is to give smaller firms a protected lane.

Clark Hill noted in its October 2026 analysis that the congressional criticism wasn't just procedural. Some members were specifically concerned that threshold increases of this magnitude, in some categories topping 1,000%, would dilute the competitive advantage that true small businesses rely on. The 60-day extension was partly a response to that pressure.

The Washington Technology reporting from November 6, 2026 captured the SBA's own position: an agency official publicly urged industry to submit comments, acknowledging that the agency needs sector-specific feedback to understand real-world impact. That's not typical bureaucratic language. It signals genuine uncertainty inside the agency about whether the proposed thresholds are calibrated correctly.

## How the Thresholds Could Shift: A Look at the Stakes

The practical stakes differ a lot depending on your industry and your current size. Here's a simplified illustration of what the structural change means for businesses near existing thresholds.

| Situation | Under Current Standards | Under Proposed Standards (If Finalized) |
|---|---|---|
| Firm just above current revenue cap | Not eligible for SBA loans or set-asides | May qualify as small |
| Mid-sized firm in a 1,000%+ threshold industry | Clearly not small | Could newly qualify as small |
| Existing small firm near threshold ceiling | Qualifies; limited larger competition | Faces more competition from newly eligible firms |
| Small contractor with active certifications | Certifications valid under current rules | No change until final rule issued |

The last row is the one that matters most right now. Baker Donelson's October 2026 guidance was clear on this: businesses should not change size certifications or self-representations based solely on the proposed thresholds. The proposed rule is not the final rule. Making premature changes to your certifications could create compliance problems with no corresponding benefit.

## What You Should Actually Do Before Thursday

If you're a federal contractor, this is the week to act. You don't need to hire a lawyer to submit a comment, though for contractors with significant set-aside revenue at stake, professional guidance is worth the conversation. The Federal Register docket is open, and the SBA has said publicly it wants industry voices.

The most useful comments are specific. If you operate in an industry where the proposed threshold increase is dramatic, explain what that change means for your competitive position in concrete terms. What contracts are you currently winning because you qualify as small? Who are the firms you'd now be competing against? Numbers and examples carry more weight than general objections.

If you're a business that's currently too large to qualify but might cross into eligibility under the new thresholds, the calculus is different. You have an interest in seeing the rule finalized. You also have an interest in understanding exactly which NAICS codes apply to your business and whether the proposed thresholds would actually change your status. Getting that wrong has real consequences.

## Don't Assume This Becomes Final Quickly

Rulemaking at this scale takes time. The SBA has to review all comments received, reconcile the significant disagreement in the record, and potentially revise the proposed thresholds before issuing a final rule. With roughly 1,200 comments already received as of mid-October, and more coming in through November 20, the agency has a substantial review ahead of it.

There's no guarantee the proposed thresholds survive intact. Some could increase. Some could be pulled back. The Office of Advocacy's lower benefit estimate, combined with congressional pressure, creates a real possibility that the final rule looks meaningfully different from what was published in August.

Stay current on SBA guidance and, if contracts or loan eligibility are material to your business, talk to a qualified attorney or CPA before making any decisions based on what this rule might become. What's on the table now is a proposal, not a mandate.
