If you're reading this, there's a decent chance someone already pitched you a merchant cash advance. Maybe it was an email, a phone call, or a pop-up ad that promised "$50,000 in 24 hours, no credit check required." And part of you was tempted, because you needed the money and the bank said no, or the bank said yes but wanted six weeks and a stack of paperwork you didn't have time to gather.

Here's what I tell people in that moment: slow down. Not because MCAs are always wrong, but because most business owners I've seen get burned by them didn't fully understand what they were signing. That's not a character flaw. The contracts are deliberately complicated, the marketing is deliberately simple, and the cost structure is unlike anything else in finance.

Let me walk you through what's actually going on under the hood.

## What a Merchant Cash Advance Actually Is

A merchant cash advance isn't a loan. That's not a technicality. It matters enormously.

When you take an MCA, a funder gives you a lump sum of cash in exchange for a percentage of your future sales, or more commonly now, a fixed daily or weekly withdrawal from your bank account. Because it's structured as a purchase of future receivables and not a loan, it sits outside most of the regulations that govern traditional lending. There's no APR disclosure required. No usury law cap in many states. No Truth in Lending Act protection.

The cost is expressed as a "factor rate," usually somewhere between 1.1 and 1.5. So if you borrow $50,000 at a factor rate of 1.4, you repay $70,000. That sounds manageable until you realize that $70,000 might be due in six months, at which point you're looking at an effective APR north of 80%, sometimes over 150%. I've seen factor rates on renewal MCAs push the equivalent APR past 200%. The [IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-employees) won't help you here, and neither will the fine print on the MCA funder's website. You have to do the math yourself, or have someone do it for you.

**The cost isn't just high. It compounds in a specific, dangerous way.**

Because most MCAs now use fixed daily ACH withdrawals rather than a percentage of sales, your repayment doesn't slow down when business slows down. You agreed to pay $800 a day regardless of what came in the door. A bad week doesn't give you a breathing room. That rigidity is what turns a short-term cash crunch into a spiral. You can't make payroll, so you take a second MCA to cover the first one. I've sat across from business owners who were stacked three deep in MCAs, paying a combined $2,400 a day out of their business account before a single employee got paid or a single vendor got called.

That practice, called stacking, is technically prohibited in most MCA contracts. Lenders often require you to disclose existing MCAs, and many will deny you if you have one already open. But the market is fragmented enough that plenty of funders don't check rigorously. If you're thinking about stacking, understand that you're not solving a cash flow problem. You're financing your way into a deeper one.

**The confession of judgment clause.**

Read your contract before you sign it. Specifically, look for a confession of judgment. This is a legal provision that lets the funder obtain a court judgment against you without first notifying you or giving you a chance to contest it. They have your signature saying you agree to the judgment in advance. Several states (New York finally restricted them for commercial loans in 2019, though commercial lending carve-outs still apply in complex ways) still allow some form of this, and in practice it means a funder can freeze your bank account before you even know you're in default.

The [U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/) has published guidance encouraging business owners to consider all financing alternatives before taking on high-cost products, and honestly, this is a good part of why.

**The renewal trap.**

Here's how a lot of MCA funnels actually work. You take a $30,000 advance. You repay it over five months. The day you finish repaying, your phone rings. The funder offers you $40,000 at another factor rate because you "have a great repayment history." And you might be tempted, because things are still tight. Each renewal resets the clock, and each renewal extracts another round of fees. I've watched businesses that were marginally profitable get completely hollowed out over two or three renewal cycles.

## When an MCA Might Actually Make Sense

I'm not here to tell you MCAs are never appropriate. That would be dishonest.

If you have a genuinely short-term, high-return opportunity (a big wholesale order you need to fulfill, a piece of equipment that breaks in your busiest season) and you have a clear, specific plan to repay the advance quickly, the math can sometimes work. The faster you repay, the lower your effective cost. That's the one scenario where a factor rate of 1.2 repaid in 90 days starts to look more like expensive credit card debt than a predatory trap.

But that scenario describes a small fraction of MCA borrowers. Most are taking advances to cover operating shortfalls. That's not a recoverable revenue bet. That's borrowing at 100% APR to pay regular bills, and that doesn't end well in most situations.

Before you go the MCA route, seriously look at SBA microloans (the SBA 7(a) program goes up to $5 million, though smaller amounts are faster to process), equipment financing, invoice factoring if you have B2B receivables, and even business credit cards. A business card at 24% APR is expensive. It's a lot less expensive than most MCAs.
