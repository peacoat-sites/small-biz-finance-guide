## What Bonus Depreciation Actually Is (And Why the Phase-Down Matters)

For years, bonus depreciation was the tax planning equivalent of a gift. Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, businesses could deduct 100% of the cost of qualifying assets in the year they were placed in service, rather than depreciating them over five, seven, or fifteen years the normal way. Buy a $60,000 piece of equipment in December, wipe out $60,000 of taxable income that same year. It was genuinely powerful.

But that 100% rate was never meant to last forever. The TCJA built in a scheduled phase-down: 80% for 2023, 60% for 2024, 40% for 2025, 20% for 2026, and then zero starting in 2027 (unless Congress acts, which it might). So if you bought a $60,000 piece of equipment in 2024, you can take 60% as bonus depreciation ($36,000) in year one, then depreciate the remaining $24,000 over the asset's normal recovery period. Still useful. Just not what it was.

What most people don't realize is that the phase-down affects planning decisions that go well beyond just equipment purchases. Vehicles, off-the-shelf software, qualified improvement property, and certain used assets that are new to your business all potentially qualify. The stakes of getting the timing wrong have gone up as the rate has come down.

Not every purchase qualifies, and this is where I see a lot of people get tripped up. The asset generally has to be:

- Tangible personal property with a recovery period of 20 years or less (think machinery, computers, furniture, certain vehicles)
- Depreciable under MACRS
- Used in your trade or business
- New to you (used property has qualified since 2017, as long as you or a related party didn't previously own it)

Qualified improvement property (QIP) also qualifies. That covers interior improvements to nonresidential buildings you don't own, which matters a lot for restaurant, retail, and office tenants doing build-outs.

Real property with a recovery period longer than 20 years, inventory, and land do not qualify. Neither does property placed in service under a written binding contract entered into before September 27, 2017 (that's an old edge case, but it occasionally comes up with long-term equipment leases or construction contracts).

One thing that catches people off guard: vehicles. Luxury auto limits under Section 179 and bonus depreciation interact in ways that aren't intuitive, and SUVs over 6,000 lbs get treated differently than passenger cars. If you're planning a significant vehicle purchase, get your CPA on the phone before you sign anything. The difference in your deduction can be substantial depending on the vehicle's GVWR, your business-use percentage, and how you combine Section 179 with bonus depreciation.

## Section 179 Is Still 100% (And How It Fits In)

Here's something that doesn't get enough attention in the bonus depreciation conversation. Section 179 still allows 100% first-year expensing in 2024, up to $1,220,000 for the year (that limit phases out dollar-for-dollar once total asset additions exceed $3,050,000). For most small businesses, Section 179 is still the first tool to reach for.

The practical difference between the two: Section 179 can't create a loss. If your business income is $50,000 and you want to use Section 179 to expense $80,000 worth of equipment, you're capped at $50,000. The excess carries forward. Bonus depreciation has no such limitation. It can absolutely create or deepen a net operating loss, which you can then carry forward to offset future income.

So a typical small business planning sequence in 2024 looks something like this: max out Section 179 first, then apply bonus depreciation to whatever's left. Or if you're in a loss year already and want the NOL to carry, lean on bonus depreciation from the start. Your CPA should be modeling both scenarios before year-end, not just defaulting to one.

I've seen more than a few business owners skip Section 179 entirely because they heard "bonus depreciation" in conversation and assumed it was the same thing, or the better thing. They're different tools with different rules, and using them together strategically is where the real planning happens.

## Timing, Placed-in-Service Rules, and the Year-End Rush

The 60% rate applies to property "placed in service" in 2024, not just purchased. That's a meaningful distinction. If you order a piece of equipment in December but it doesn't arrive and get installed until January 2025, it's a 2025 asset subject to the 40% rate. This matters more than people think during Q4 planning.

For something like software or a laptop, placed-in-service is usually the date it's available and ready to use. For machinery that requires installation, it's when installation is complete and the equipment is operational. Get clarity on this from your vendor if you're cutting it close to December 31.

The [U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA)](https://www.sba.gov/) has solid general guidance on tax planning for small businesses, but for year-end depreciation timing specifically, you really want a CPA who knows your books.

## The 2025 Situation and Whether to Accelerate Purchases

This is the question I'm getting most often right now. Should you pull 2025 equipment purchases into 2024 to capture 60% instead of 40%?

Maybe. The honest answer is that it depends on your current-year income, your cash position, your interest rate on any financing, and whether you even have taxable income worth sheltering. Accelerating a deduction is only valuable if you have income to offset. If 2024 is a low-income year for you and 2025 looks strong, waiting might actually serve you better, especially if you expect to be in a higher bracket next year.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) are worth bookmarking for the broader financial picture, but the acceleration question is squarely a tax planning call that belongs with your accountant.

There is also ongoing conversation in Congress about reinstating 100% bonus depreciation retroactively, which has happened before. I wouldn't count on it, and I wouldn't make major cash flow decisions based on legislative hope, but it's worth keeping an eye on as a potential planning variable heading into 2025.

### FAQ

#### Does bonus depreciation apply to used equipment in 2024?

Yes, used equipment can qualify for the 60% bonus depreciation in 2024 as long as the property is new to you and was not previously owned by you or a related party. It also can't have been acquired in a like-kind exchange or from a related party transaction.

#### Can bonus depreciation create a tax loss for my business?

It can. Unlike Section 179, bonus depreciation is not limited to your business income. If your deductions exceed your income in 2024, you'll have a net operating loss that you can carry forward to future tax years under current law.

#### What's the bonus depreciation rate if I missed the 2024 deadline?

Property placed in service in 2025 qualifies at 40%, dropping to 20% in 2026, and going to zero in 2027 under current law. Congress could extend or reinstate the higher rates, but no change has been signed into law as of now.

#### Is bonus depreciation the same as Section 179 expensing?

No, though they accomplish similar things. Section 179 lets you deduct 100% of qualifying assets up to $1,220,000 in 2024 but can't create a loss. Bonus depreciation in 2024 is capped at 60% but has no income limitation. Most small businesses benefit from using both strategically.

#### Do I have to take bonus depreciation, or can I opt out?

You can opt out. The election to not claim bonus depreciation is made on a class-by-class basis on your tax return. There are situations where declining it makes sense, like when you expect significantly higher income in future years and want to preserve deductions. Talk to your CPA before assuming you should always take the maximum available depreciation.
