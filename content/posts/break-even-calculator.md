Two of the most common financial questions small business owners and freelancers face are: *How much do I need to sell to break even?* and *How much will I owe in self-employment taxes?* These two questions sit at the heart of every business plan and every quarterly tax payment. Getting them right lets you price products with confidence and avoid unpleasant surprises in April.

## Break-Even Analysis Explained

A break-even analysis separates your costs into two buckets:

**Fixed costs** are the expenses you pay regardless of how many units you sell — rent, insurance, software subscriptions, salaried staff, and loan payments. These do not change month to month based on production volume.

**Variable costs** are the costs that rise directly with each unit produced or sold — raw materials, packaging, per-transaction merchant fees, and hourly labor tied to production. The key formula is:

> **Break-Even Units = Fixed Monthly Costs ÷ Contribution Margin per Unit**
> **Contribution Margin = Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit**

For example, if your fixed costs are $5,000/month, you sell a product for $45, and each unit costs $15 to produce, your contribution margin is $30 and your break-even point is 167 units per month. Sell 168 units and you are in the black; sell 166 and you take a loss.

The **contribution margin percentage** (contribution margin ÷ price) is equally important. A 67% CM means 67 cents of every revenue dollar flows toward fixed costs and eventual profit. Businesses with high fixed costs and low CM% face steep break-even points that require significant sales volume.

## Self-Employment Tax: What Solo Business Owners Must Know

If you operate as a sole proprietor, single-member LLC, or partnership, you are responsible for paying both the employee AND employer portions of Social Security and Medicare taxes — together called **self-employment (SE) tax**. This catches many new entrepreneurs off guard because W-2 employees only see the employee half (7.65%) withheld from their paycheck; the employer pays the other 7.65% invisibly.

As a self-employed person, you pay both halves — 15.3% total. However, the IRS applies this rate to only **92.35%** of your net profit (you get a deduction equal to the employer-equivalent half, which reduces your net earnings subject to SE tax). You can then deduct **half the SE tax** as an above-the-line deduction on Schedule 1, reducing your adjusted gross income.

## Planning for Quarterly Estimated Taxes

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal taxes, the IRS requires you to pay **quarterly estimated taxes** (Form 1040-ES), due in April, June, September, and January. Failing to pay can result in underpayment penalties.

Use the SE tax figure from the calculator above as your baseline. Add your estimated federal income tax (based on your bracket) to arrive at total quarterly payments. Many self-employed business owners find it helpful to open a separate savings account and transfer 25–30% of every client payment into it immediately — when quarterly payments come due, the money is already set aside.
