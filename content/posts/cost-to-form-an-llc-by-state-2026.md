Most people spend more time picking their LLC name than they do researching the actual cost to form one. Then they get surprised by a $300 annual report fee they weren't expecting, or they pay a registered agent $200 a year when a $49 option would've done exactly the same job.

Let me just show you the real numbers.

Filing fees to form an LLC range from $35 in Kentucky to $500 in Massachusetts, as of August 2026. That's a fourteen-to-one ratio for the same legal structure. And the filing fee is often the cheapest part of the equation once you factor in annual fees, franchise taxes, and the occasional state-specific surcharge that nobody tells you about until you're already in.

## The Filing Fee Is Just the Entry Ticket

Here's what most people don't realize: the state filing fee is a one-time cost, but the ongoing annual costs are what actually determine whether your state choice matters financially. I've seen clients form in Wyoming because they read a blog post about low fees, then get blindsided when they realized they still needed to register as a foreign LLC in their home state (where they actually operate), pay that state's fees, and pay Wyoming's too. Two sets of fees, two registered agents. Not the deal they thought they were getting.

California and New York deserve their own conversation because they're both popular and genuinely punishing in ways that the quick-google answer misses.

California charges every LLC a minimum $800 annual franchise tax, due even if your business made zero dollars. No exemption for new businesses in their first year as of 2026 (that temporary exemption expired). A reader named Marcus emailed me last spring after forming a California LLC for a side project he hadn't launched yet. He owed $800 before earning a single dollar. If you're a California resident running a small or part-time business, that $800 floor is real money and worth factoring into whether you even need an LLC yet.

New York has the publication requirement, which I'd argue is the most archaic business formation rule still on the books. You have to publish a notice of formation in two newspapers for six consecutive weeks in the county where your LLC is located. In Manhattan and some other downstate counties, that can run $1,200 to $2,000. Albany and upstate counties are cheaper, sometimes under $200 total. The state filing fee is $200, which looks fine until you add the publication cost.

Nevada gets oversold as a tax haven constantly. Yes, Nevada has no state corporate income tax. But Nevada's annual fees for an LLC (the State Business License plus the Annual List) currently run around $350 a year. If you actually live in, say, Arizona, you'll also register your Nevada LLC as a foreign entity in Arizona and pay Arizona's fees too. You're not avoiding your home state's rules by filing elsewhere. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) are worth checking if you're trying to understand what financial disclosures apply to your structure regardless of formation state.

## What Registered Agent Fees Actually Cost You

Every LLC needs a registered agent in its state of formation. If you form in your home state and you have a physical address there, you can be your own registered agent for free. Most people don't know that.

If you form in a state where you don't live (Delaware, Wyoming, Nevada), you need to pay someone to be your registered agent there. Prices range from $49 to $299 a year depending on the service. Northwest Registered Agent currently runs $125/year, which is what I'd call the sweet spot for reliability without overpaying. Registered Agents Inc. has an $89/year option that works fine for straightforward situations.

Worked example: a Colorado freelancer forms an LLC in Wyoming thinking they'll save money.

Scenario: $100 Wyoming filing fee + $60 Wyoming annual fee + $125 registered agent = $285/year in Wyoming costs alone. Then Colorado foreign LLC registration at $100 + $10/year. Total first year: $495. If they'd just formed in Colorado: $50 + $10/year = $60. The Wyoming route costs $435 more in year one, and the ongoing savings from Wyoming's lack of income tax are zero because Colorado income tax applies to where they live and work anyway.

I've run this comparison for dozens of clients and the math almost never favors out-of-state formation for a single-member LLC with no investment ambitions.

## When Delaware Actually Makes Sense

Delaware is genuinely worth it in specific situations: you're [raising venture capital](/bootstrapping-vs-raising-venture-capital-for-startups/), you plan to issue multiple classes of shares, or you have sophisticated investors who just expect a Delaware entity. Delaware's Court of Chancery has 200 years of case law that makes it the most predictable jurisdiction for business disputes. That matters when real money is involved.

For a solo consultant, an Etsy seller, or a two-person service business? Delaware adds cost and complexity with essentially no benefit. I'd go straight to your home state.

## The Costs Nobody Puts in the Headline

A few things that regularly catch people off guard:

Publication fees (New York, as covered). Some formation services charge "state fees" that include their markup without disclosing it clearly. Always compare what they quote against your state's SOS website directly. Tennessee's annual LLC fee is tied to member count, not revenue: $300 per member, with a $300 minimum. A 3-member Tennessee LLC pays $900 a year just in state fees. Maryland's $300 annual personal property return fee is steep relative to its $100 filing fee. And Arizona has no annual report at all, which nobody advertises loudly enough.

If you're handling your own books after formation, Mike Piper's [*Accounting Made Simple*](https://www.amazon.com/Accounting-Made-Simple-Explained-Pages/dp/0981454224?tag=contentportfo-20) is a genuinely useful $10 paperback. And yes, please talk to a CPA before you file, especially regarding tax elections like S-corp status, which can be made on top of your LLC and affect your actual tax bill more than the state filing fee ever will.
