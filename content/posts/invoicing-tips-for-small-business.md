You send the invoice. Then you wait. A week goes by. Then two. You follow up, feeling awkward about it, and the client responds with "Oh, I didn't get that" or "We'll process it next cycle." Meanwhile, your rent is due and your cash flow looks like a heart monitor after a sprint. If you've been in business more than six months, you know this feeling. And the frustrating part is that most of the pain is preventable.

Invoicing isn't glamorous. Nobody starts a business dreaming about billing software and payment terms. But your invoice is the document that actually turns your work into money. Getting it wrong, or just being sloppy about it, costs real dollars and real time.

## What Your Invoice Actually Needs to Include

A lot of small business owners send invoices that are technically incomplete. They miss line items, forget to specify payment terms, or use a template they pulled from somewhere without really reading it. Then they wonder why clients are slow to pay or dispute charges.

At minimum, a professional invoice should include:

- Your business name, address, and contact information
- The client's full name or company name and billing address
- A unique invoice number (this matters more than you think for your records)
- The invoice date and the due date, spelled out clearly
- An itemized list of services or products, with quantities and unit prices
- The total amount due, including any applicable taxes
- Accepted payment methods
- Any late fee policy, stated clearly

That last one trips people up. If you plan to charge a late fee, you have to tell the client upfront, ideally in your contract and again on the invoice. You can't just add it after the fact and expect it to stick.

I've seen clients lose disputes with customers simply because their invoice didn't state net-30 terms anywhere. The client assumed they had 60 days. Put the due date in plain language: "Payment due by March 15, 2025." Don't just write "Net 30" and assume everyone knows what that means.

## Set Payment Terms That Actually Work for Your Cash Flow

"Net 30" became the default in B2B invoicing almost by accident. It's not a law. It's a convention, and it's one that often works against small businesses that have their own obligations to meet.

Think about what net 30 means in practice. You complete the work, send the invoice, and then wait up to 30 days to get paid. If your client is slow to open their mail or process bills, you might be waiting 45 days or more. That's your money sitting in someone else's bank account.

Here's what I recommend instead:

**For service businesses with project-based work:** Require a deposit before you start, typically 25 to 50 percent. The remaining balance is due on delivery or within 7 to 14 days of completion. This protects you and creates a financial incentive for the client to stay engaged in the project.

**For ongoing retainer clients:** Bill at the beginning of the month, not the end. You're providing access to your time and expertise. There's no reason to float that cost for 30 days after the fact.

**For product businesses or larger orders:** Net 15 is often entirely reasonable, especially for smaller customers. Save net 30 for established clients with a good payment history or for situations where the client's size gives them leverage.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources touch on cash flow management in ways that reinforce this point: getting paid faster is almost always more valuable than accommodating a customer's preference for delayed payment.

You can also experiment with early payment discounts. Something like "2/10 net 30" means the client gets a 2 percent discount if they pay within 10 days. Some clients love this. Others ignore it. It's worth trying if you're dealing with a client who has cash but just moves slowly.

## Use Software, Not a Word Doc

If you're still sending invoices built in Microsoft Word or piecing them together in a spreadsheet, stop. Not because there's anything morally wrong with it, but because it's slowing you down, creating errors, and making your books harder to reconcile.

Invoicing software, even basic free tools, handles things Word simply can't. It tracks whether an invoice has been opened. It sends automatic reminders. It logs payment dates. It calculates totals and tax automatically. And it connects to your accounting records so you're not re-entering data.

QuickBooks Online, FreshBooks, and Wave (which is free) are the three I see most often with small business clients. Wave is genuinely solid for freelancers and very small operations. QuickBooks is the gold standard if you're eventually going to hand your books to a CPA. FreshBooks sits in the middle and has a clean interface that non-accountants tend to like.

## A Step-by-Step Process for Following Up on Unpaid Invoices

This is where most small business owners lose money, not because the client refuses to pay, but because the owner doesn't have a follow-up system and the invoice just gets forgotten.

Here's a simple process that works:

**Step 1: Send the invoice immediately.** Don't wait until the end of the week or the end of the month. Invoice the same day the work is completed, or the same day the product ships.

**Step 2: Set an automatic reminder in your invoicing software.** Most tools let you schedule a "friendly reminder" email at 3 to 5 days before the due date. This isn't nagging. It's good process.

**Step 3: Follow up by phone the day after the due date.** Not by email. Phone. A quick two-minute call that says "Hi, I wanted to check that everything looks good with invoice #1042 from two weeks ago" almost always gets faster results than another email.

**Step 4: Send a formal overdue notice at 7 days past due.** This email should be polite but direct. Reference the invoice number, the amount, the original due date, and any late fee that now applies.

**Step 5: At 30 days past due, escalate.** This means a more formal written notice referencing your contract, a possible hold on future services, and potentially a conversation about a payment plan. If the amount is significant, this is also the point where you consult a collections attorney or consider small claims court.

I want to be honest about something here. Most late payments don't require lawyers. They require consistency. Clients who know you'll follow up reliably tend to prioritize your invoices over vendors who go quiet.

## How Your Invoice Language Affects How Fast You Get Paid

This one surprises people. Research has shown that the specific words on an invoice can influence payment speed. A 2013 study by FreshBooks found that invoices with "please" and "thank you" language were paid several days faster than those without it. That's a small thing with a real impact.

Beyond politeness, be direct about consequences. "A late fee of 1.5 percent per month will apply to balances unpaid after 30 days" is clear. "Invoices are subject to additional charges if not paid promptly" is vague and unenforceable.

Also consider how you describe your work. Vague line items like "consulting services" invite questions. Specific descriptions like "Brand strategy session, March 4, 2025, 2 hours" leave nothing to dispute. The more clearly your invoice explains what the client got, the harder it is to delay payment with "I need to look into this first."

Your payment instructions should be equally clear. Don't just say "check or ACH." List your exact preferred method, provide your bank routing and account number or a payment link, and if you accept credit cards, say so explicitly. Friction in the payment process is friction between you and your money.

## Invoicing and Taxes: What You Need to Know

Your invoices are financial records, and they matter at tax time. How you invoice affects how you track revenue, what you owe, and when you owe it, especially if you're on an accrual accounting basis versus cash basis.

On a cash basis, which most small businesses use, income counts when you actually receive payment. So a December invoice paid in January is January income.

On an accrual basis, income counts when the invoice is issued, regardless of when you're paid. This matters if you're a larger operation or if your lender or investors want GAAP-compliant financials.

Sales tax is the other variable. If you sell physical products or certain taxable services, you may be required to collect and remit sales tax, and your invoice needs to reflect that accurately. This is highly state-specific. Get this wrong and you're looking at back taxes and penalties. Please work with a CPA to make sure your invoicing reflects your actual tax obligations. This is not an area to guess about.

## FAQ

### How early should I send an invoice after completing work?

The same day, ideally. The longer you wait, the longer the payment cycle starts. If you complete a project on a Tuesday, invoice Tuesday afternoon. Waiting until end of month is a habit that costs you cash flow and sometimes the invoice itself gets lost in a pile of other end-of-month bills.

### What should I do if a client disputes an invoice?

Stay calm and stay factual. Ask them to specify exactly what they're disputing and why. Reference your contract, your scope of work, and any written approvals they gave during the project. In most cases, disputes come down to unclear expectations, not bad faith. Resolve what you can in writing, and if the amount is large enough to matter, involve a business attorney before making concessions.

### Is it legal to charge late fees?

Yes, in most cases, but you need to have disclosed the policy in advance. A late fee buried in fine print on the invoice itself, applied the first time, is a lot weaker than a late fee referenced in your signed contract and noted at the top of every invoice. Laws on maximum allowable late fees vary by state, so confirm what's permissible where you operate.

### Should I require a deposit from new clients?

Yes. Almost always. A deposit, typically 25 to 50 percent upfront, does three things: it filters out clients who aren't serious, it covers your initial costs and time, and it creates a psychological commitment that makes the client more engaged in the project. I've rarely seen a client who refused a reasonable deposit become a great long-term customer.
