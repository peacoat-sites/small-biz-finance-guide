Recent investigative reporting has put a name and a number to a problem many small-business owners already feel in their gut: for example, a business owner who took what looked like a quick funding solution can end up hundreds of thousands or even millions of dollars in debt, trapped in a product called a [merchant cash advance](/merchant-cash-advance-risks/). The story spread fast, and for good reason. MCAs are now the fastest-growing source of small-business funding in the country, and they carry almost no federal oversight. That combination is worth understanding before you ever sign one.

The timing couldn't be worse, or more clarifying. The CFPB finalized its Section 1071 rule, which governs federal collection of small-[business lending](/current-business-lending-rates/) data.  What it did, quietly, was cement merchant cash advances as explicitly excluded from that data collection. So right as awareness of MCA risks is peaking, the federal government formally stepped back from monitoring the product. 

Small-business bankruptcy filings have risen in recent years, driven by inflation and tightening conventional credit. That pressure is pushing more owners toward high-cost alternatives. MCAs are often the first place they land.

## What an MCA Actually Is (and Why It Isn't a Loan)

A merchant cash advance is not a loan. That sentence matters legally. An MCA is structured as the purchase of your future receivables. A funder gives you a lump sum today, and in exchange you agree to sell them a fixed percentage of your daily or weekly sales until a larger predetermined amount is paid back.

Because it's a sale of future revenue rather than a credit transaction, it falls outside the Truth in Lending Act and the CFPB's Regulation Z. No APR disclosure is required. The funder doesn't have to tell you what your annualized cost is. They quote you a "factor rate" instead, something like 1.3 or 1.49, which means you receive $100,000 and pay back $130,000 or $149,000. That sounds manageable until you realize repayment often happens over just 4 to 8 months, which makes the annualized cost enormous.

There's no federal law requiring them to show you that math.

## The Oversight Gap Is Real and Growing

As of late 2026, only 11 states have passed any MCA disclosure laws, including California, New York, Texas, and Florida. The other 39 states have nothing. No requirement to show cost, no APR equivalent, no standardized terms. According to reporting from deBanked, the CFPB's Section 1071 exclusion wasn't a surprise to industry watchers, but its timing, right as MCA volume is surging, made it a significant moment.

The result is a funding product that's growing fast, in widespread use, and largely invisible to regulators at both the federal and state level for most borrowers. If you're in one of the 39 states without disclosure laws, you are negotiating on your own.

## The Real Cost: A Side-by-Side Look

The gap between MCA costs and conventional small-[business credit](/business-credit-card-vs-personal-credit-card-for-llc/) is wide. Here's how the numbers compare on a $100,000 funding need across common options available to owners in late 2026.

| Funding Type | Typical Cost on $100K | Repayment Term | APR Disclosure Required? |
|---|---|---|---|
| SBA 7(a) Loan | ~$12,000–$18,000 total interest | 7–10 years | Yes |
| Bank term loan | ~$15,000–$25,000 total interest | 3–5 years | Yes |
| Online term lender | ~$20,000–$40,000 total interest | 1–3 years | Yes |
| Merchant Cash Advance | ~$30,000–$60,000+ in fees | 4–12 months | No |

The SBA 7(a) option is worth modeling before you assume you won't qualify. You can estimate real SBA loan payments and total costs using this [SBA loan calculator](/sba-loan-calculator/) before talking to any lender.

The MCA column isn't showing worst-case numbers. A factor rate of 1.3 to 1.49 on a 6-month repayment schedule translates to an effective annualized rate that can run well above 60% and sometimes above 100%. The funders don't have to tell you that.

## Who Gets Hurt and How

The NPR investigation profiled how the structure of an MCA can accelerate a business into a debt spiral. When sales slow down, some MCA contracts include fixed daily ACH withdrawals rather than a true percentage of sales, so the repayment doesn't slow with your revenue. Miss a payment or fall behind, and some contracts allow "confession of judgment" clauses in states where they're still legal, which let the funder get a court judgment against you without first serving you notice.

Stacking is the other big hazard. Because MCAs aren't reported to credit bureaus in a consistent way, some owners take a second or third advance to cover the daily withdrawals from the first. That's how a manageable cash crunch becomes a seven-figure problem.

Rising small-business bankruptcy filings tell part of the story. Owners who end up in bankruptcy court frequently list multiple MCA agreements in their filings.

## What to Do Before You Sign Anything

If you're being pitched an MCA, ask the funder to give you the total payback amount, the daily or weekly withdrawal amount, and the expected repayment term in writing. Then calculate the annualized cost yourself. Divide the total fee by the principal, divide that by the repayment period in months, and multiply by 12. That's your rough APR equivalent.

Compare that number against what an SBA loan or even a higher-rate online term lender would actually cost. The comparison often changes the decision.

Also ask whether your contract includes a fixed daily ACH or a true percentage of sales, whether there's a confession of judgment clause, and what happens if you want to pay it off early (many MCAs don't offer meaningful prepayment savings because the full factor rate is baked in regardless).

If you're in one of the 39 states without disclosure laws, you won't get these answers automatically. You have to ask, in writing, before signing. Getting a second opinion from an independent small-business attorney or CPA before signing an MCA is worth the cost of an hour of their time.

Recent investigative reporting and ongoing CFPB rulemaking have together drawn a clear picture: the public now has enough information to treat MCAs with the seriousness they deserve. Fast money is real. So is the price.
