Most small business owners I work with are leaving somewhere between $5,000 and $30,000 a year in tax deductions on the table. Not because they're doing anything wrong. Because nobody told them the Solo 401(k) existed.

I've seen this play out dozens of times. A self-employed consultant, freelancer, or single-member LLC owner is diligently stashing money into a regular IRA every year, proud of themselves for saving anything at all. Then they find out their contribution limit was $7,000 when it could have been $66,000. That's a painful conversation to have in a tax planning meeting. Let's make sure you're not in that room.

## What a Solo 401(k) Actually Is

The Solo 401(k) goes by a few names: individual 401(k), self-employed 401(k), one-participant 401(k). The IRS calls it a one-participant 401(k) plan, and you can find the full framework over at the [IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) if you want the primary source. The concept is straightforward. It's a 401(k) plan designed specifically for business owners with no employees other than themselves (and optionally, a spouse).

Here's what makes it different from every other retirement account available to the self-employed: you get to contribute as both the employee *and* the employer. Two separate contribution buckets. That's the whole reason the limits are so dramatically higher than an IRA.

For 2024, you can contribute up to $23,000 as the "employee" (that's your elective deferral, same as a W-2 worker at a company would contribute). Then, as the "employer," you can add a profit-sharing contribution of up to 25% of your net self-employment compensation. The combined max across both buckets is $69,000 for 2024, or $76,500 if you're 50 or older (that's the $7,500 catch-up contribution added in).

To be eligible, you need self-employment income and no full-time employees. If you hire someone who works more than 1,000 hours a year, you'd need to offer them a plan too, which is a different situation entirely.

Let's say you're a self-employed graphic designer making $120,000 in net income. With a traditional IRA, your 2024 deductible contribution caps at $7,000. With a Solo 401(k), depending on your net self-employment income after the self-employment tax deduction, you might be able to contribute $23,000 as the employee portion alone, then add a profit-sharing contribution on top. We're potentially talking about a deduction that's four to five times larger than an IRA would give you.

What that means in real money depends on your tax bracket, and I'd always recommend running these numbers with a CPA before making decisions. But at a 24% federal rate, the difference between a $7,000 IRA deduction and a $40,000 Solo 401(k) deduction is roughly $7,920 in federal taxes saved in a single year. Every year.

There's also a Roth option at many providers. You contribute after-tax dollars to the employee deferral portion, growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. If you expect to be in a higher bracket later (or you're younger and have decades of compounding ahead), the Roth Solo 401(k) is worth serious consideration.

## How to Actually Set One Up

This is where a lot of people get stuck, so here's a practical walkthrough.

**Step 1: Choose a provider.** Fidelity, Vanguard, and Charles Schwab all offer Solo 401(k) plans with no annual fees and solid investment options. Fidelity and Schwab allow Roth contributions; Vanguard's individual 401(k) doesn't currently offer a Roth option, which is a real limitation if that matters to you. If you want loan provisions or alternative investments (real estate, for instance), you'd look at a self-directed Solo 401(k) through providers like Equity Trust or Rocket Dollar, though those come with fees and more administrative complexity.

**Step 2: Open the account before December 31.** The plan must be established by December 31 of the tax year you want to use it for. This is the deadline that catches people. The actual contributions can be made up to your tax filing deadline (including extensions), but the plan itself has to exist by year-end.

**Step 3: Get an EIN for the plan.** You'll need a separate Employer Identification Number for the 401(k) plan, even if you already have one for your business. This is free through the IRS and takes about five minutes online.

**Step 4: Complete the adoption agreement.** Every provider has their own documents. It's not complicated, but read the contribution options carefully. You want to confirm whether your plan allows Roth contributions, loans, and after-tax contributions if any of those features matter to you.

**Step 5: Track your contributions separately.** Employee deferrals and employer profit-sharing contributions are reported differently on your taxes. Keep clean records. Your accountant will thank you.

Once your plan assets hit $250,000, you'll need to file Form 5500-EZ annually with the IRS. Below that threshold, no annual filing required. Simple.

## A Note on the Spouse Provision

If your spouse works in the business (even part-time, as long as they earn W-2 or self-employment income from it), they can also contribute to the Solo 401(k). That potentially doubles everything: two employee deferral contributions, two employer contributions. For a couple running a business together, this is one of the most powerful tax reduction strategies I know of. Worth asking about specifically if this is your situation.

## What Most People Get Wrong

A few things I see come up constantly:

People confuse net self-employment income with gross revenue. Your profit-sharing contribution is based on net earned income, after expenses and after the deductible portion of self-employment tax. The exact calculation is a little circular mathematically, and most tax software handles it automatically, but if you're trying to figure out your max contribution on a napkin, know that 25% of net compensation doesn't mean 25% of your top-line revenue.

People also wait too long. I can't count how many people have called me in January wanting to open a Solo 401(k) for last year. The plan had to be established by December 31. You can still make the contributions for that year (until your filing deadline), but only if the plan was already open. Set a calendar reminder for November.

Finally, people assume they need a lot of money to bother. You don't. Even if you can only contribute $5,000 or $10,000 in year one, establishing the plan costs nothing at Fidelity or Schwab, and the flexibility to contribute more in better years is worth having the account open.

## FAQ

### Can I have a Solo 401(k) if I also have a day job with a 401(k)?

Yes, but the employee deferral limit ($23,000 in 2024) is per person, not per plan. So if you're contributing $15,000 at your day job, you can only contribute $8,000 more in employee deferrals to your Solo 401(k). The employer profit-sharing portion is separate and not affected.

### What happens to my Solo 401(k) if I hire an employee?

Once you hire a non-spouse employee who meets the eligibility rules (generally 1,000+ hours per year and age 21+), the plan typically loses its one-participant status and you'd need to convert it to a regular 401(k) plan, which involves offering coverage to employees and additional compliance requirements. Talk to a plan administrator before hiring if this is on your radar.

### Is a SEP-IRA better than a Solo 401(k)?

For some people, yes. A SEP-IRA is easier to set up, has almost no paperwork, and allows large contributions (up to 25% of net compensation, same as the employer side of a Solo 401(k)). But it doesn't have an employee deferral component, so if your business income is lower, you can often contribute significantly more to a Solo 401(k). The SEP also doesn't allow Roth contributions. I generally lean toward the Solo 401(k) for anyone who wants flexibility, but the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has a solid comparison if you want a second read.

### Can I take a loan from my Solo 401(k)?

Some plans allow it, some don't. Fidelity's Solo 401(k), for instance, does not currently offer a loan provision. If loan access matters to you, confirm this with the provider before opening the account. Self-directed providers are more likely to offer it.

### What's the deadline to make contributions for 2024?

If you're a sole proprietor filing a Schedule C, you have until your tax filing deadline, including extensions (so October 15, 2025 if you file an extension). But again: the plan itself must have been established by December 31, 2024. Contributions and plan establishment have different deadlines, and mixing them up is a costly mistake.
