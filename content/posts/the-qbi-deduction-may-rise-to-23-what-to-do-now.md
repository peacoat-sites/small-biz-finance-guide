Most coverage of H.R. 8415 reads like a press release. "Deduction goes up, pass-through owners win, stay tuned." That doesn't help you make a decision in June 2026, when the tax year is halfway done and your strategy needs to catch up. Here's what actually matters.

Rep. David Kustoff (R-TN) introduced the Small Business Tax Cut Act in April 2026, proposing to raise the Section 199A qualified business income deduction from 20% to 23%. This sits on top of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, which already made the QBI deduction permanent. If H.R. 8415 passes, roughly 33 million pass-through businesses (sole proprietors, S-corps, partnerships, LLCs) get a slightly bigger tax break on qualified income. That 3-percentage-point difference sounds trivial. It's not.

## What the Math Actually Looks Like

Run the real numbers. A $300,000 qualified business income generates a $60,000 deduction at 20%, which saves you roughly $19,200 in taxes at a 32% marginal rate. Bump it to 23% and you're deducting $69,000, landing you around $22,080 in savings. That's nearly $3,000 more on the same revenue without changing anything.

A $500,000 QBI business sees the gap widen to almost $5,000 in extra savings. Not transformational for everyone. But for the Main Street business owner watching costs climb, that's a payroll hire, a quarterly equipment payment, or actual money in the bank.

Tax analysts also point out that a 23% deduction narrows the effective tax rate gap between pass-throughs and C corporations, which pay a flat 21%. This gap has been hotly debated since 2017, when the deduction was created. A 23% rate gets pass-through owners closer to structural parity, though not all the way there.

## The OBBBA Foundation You're Already Standing On

Before H.R. 8415 even gets voted on, 2026 already brought meaningful changes worth building into your planning right now. The OBBBA locked in several provisions affecting small businesses this year. Section 179 expensing jumped to $2.5 million, which matters if you're buying equipment, software, or vehicles before year-end. And starting in 2026, anyone with at least $1,000 in qualified business income gets a minimum $400 QBI deduction, even if they'd normally get phased out by income thresholds or W-2 wage limitations.

That minimum deduction protects newer businesses, part-time entrepreneurs, and anyone in a specified service trade squeezed by the rules. It won't move the needle for high-revenue operations, but if you've been told you don't qualify, revisit that assumption with your CPA.

The 1099-NEC reporting threshold shifted too. You now file for contractors paid $2,000 or more, up from $600. If your accounts payable was built around $600, update it now rather than scrambling in January.

## What "Gaining Momentum" Actually Means Legislatively

H.R. 8415 has Republican backing and small business advocacy groups behind it. But a standalone deduction increase isn't guaranteed to pass on its own. Congress has used QBI provisions as bargaining chips repeatedly. Kustoff announced the bill on April 23, 2026. It's getting attention. Attention and a floor vote are different things.

The realistic path is getting folded into a larger tax package, like the OBBBA did. If that happens, the effective date changes everything. Retroactive to January 2026? Your whole annual strategy shifts. January 2027? You're planning this year under 20%.

Don't build your 2026 strategy around something that hasn't happened. Do position yourself to benefit immediately if it does. Those are different moves.

## What Pass-Through Owners Should Actually Do Right Now

This isn't a waiting moment. Whether the deduction stays at 20% or rises to 23%, mid-year structural decisions have real teeth.

First: if you're an LLC taxed as a sole proprietor and your net income consistently exceeds $200,000, the S-corp election conversation is overdue. The QBI deduction interacts with W-2 wages paid, and S-corp owners can optimize the salary-to-distribution ratio in ways sole proprietors can't. A 23% deduction amplifies those benefits.

Second: the $2.5 million Section 179 limit means capital expenditure planning for the second half of 2026 deserves attention now, not December. Equipment, software licenses, and qualifying property placed in service before year-end are on the table. Don't force purchases for the deduction, but don't ignore timing either.

Third, and most people skip this: document your qualified business income properly all year long. The QBI deduction applies to net income from qualified trades, and messy bookkeeping means you'll either miss deductions or create audit exposure. This is a mid-year books review conversation, not an April scramble.

Finally: if you operate in a specified service trade (law, consulting, financial services, health), the phase-out thresholds hit you harder. At higher income levels, you might get little to no QBI deduction regardless of whether it's 20% or 23%. Know which bucket you're in before planning around a deduction you might not capture.

The QBI landscape in mid-2026 is genuinely better than three years ago, and it could improve further. The OBBBA removed the planning uncertainty that made this deduction frustrating to rely on. H.R. 8415 is real upside, if not guaranteed. Get your structure right now, work with a qualified tax advisor on your specifics, and don't let legislative noise distract you from the decisions fully in your control today.
