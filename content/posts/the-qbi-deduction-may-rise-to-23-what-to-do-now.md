Most of the coverage on H.R. 8415 reads like a press release. "Deduction goes up, pass-through owners win, stay tuned." That's not enough to make a decision in June 2026, when the tax year is already half over and your strategy needs to catch up. Here's what's actually worth understanding.

Rep. David Kustoff (R-TN) introduced the Small Business Tax Cut Act in April 2026, proposing to raise the Section 199A qualified business income deduction from 20% to 23%. This is on top of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July 4, 2025, which already made the QBI deduction permanent. If H.R. 8415 passes, an estimated 33 million pass-through businesses, sole proprietors, S-corps, partnerships, and LLCs would get a slightly bigger tax haircut off their qualified income. That 3-percentage-point difference sounds small. It is not small when you're running real numbers.

## What the Math Actually Looks Like

Let's be concrete. If your pass-through business generates $300,000 in qualified business income, the current 20% deduction saves you $60,000 in taxable income. At a 32% marginal rate, that's roughly $19,200 in tax savings. Bump the deduction to 23% and your deductible amount rises to $69,000, saving you about $22,080. That's nearly $3,000 more in your pocket on the same revenue, doing nothing differently.

For a $500,000 QBI business, the gap widens to nearly $5,000 in additional savings. These aren't transformational numbers for everyone, but for a Main Street business owner who's been watching costs climb for two years, that's a meaningful payroll contribution, a quarterly equipment payment, or actual retained cash.

Tax analysts note that a higher deduction would also narrow the effective rate gap between pass-throughs and C corporations, which pay a flat 21%. That gap has been a live debate since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act created the deduction in the first place. A 23% deduction gets pass-through owners closer to structural parity, though not all the way there.

Before H.R. 8415 even comes to a vote, 2026 already brought meaningful changes worth building into your planning. As Carr, Riggs & Ingram noted in May 2026, the OBBBA locked in several provisions that affect small businesses this year. The Section 179 expensing limit jumped to $2.5 million, which is relevant for any business looking to buy equipment, software, or vehicles before year-end. And starting in 2026, the IRS guarantees a minimum $400 QBI deduction for anyone with at least $1,000 in qualified business income, even if they'd otherwise be phased out by the income thresholds or the W-2 wage limitations.

That minimum deduction is a small but real protection for newer businesses, part-time entrepreneurs, and anyone operating in a specified service trade or profession who gets squeezed by the phase-out rules. It's not going to move the needle for high-revenue operations, but if you've been told you don't qualify, it's worth revisiting that assumption with your CPA.

The 1099-NEC reporting threshold also shifted in 2026. You now only have to file a 1099-NEC for contractors paid $2,000 or more, up from $600. ARF Financial's June 2026 analysis flagged this as one of the compliance changes small businesses need to factor into mid-year operations. If your accounts payable process was built around the $600 threshold, update it now rather than scrambling in January.

## What "Gaining Momentum" Actually Means Legislatively

Let's be honest about where H.R. 8415 sits. It has Republican support and backing from small business advocacy groups, which is a reasonable foundation. But a standalone bill that increases a deduction isn't guaranteed to pass on its own merits. Congress has repeatedly used QBI provisions as bargaining chips. Kustoff's office announced the bill on April 23, 2026, and it's been getting attention, but attention and a floor vote are different things.

The more realistic path for the 23% deduction is getting folded into a larger tax package, similar to how the OBBBA bundled numerous provisions together. If that happens, the effective date matters enormously. A retroactive-to-January-2026 provision changes your annual planning. A January-2027 effective date means this year's decisions are made under the current 20% deduction.

Don't structure your 2026 tax strategy around an outcome that hasn't happened. Do position yourself to benefit quickly if it does. Those are different postures.

## What Pass-Through Owners Should Actually Do Right Now

This is not the moment to wait. Whether the deduction stays at 20% or rises to 23%, the structural decisions you make in the middle of the year have real consequences.

First, if you're an LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship and your net income is consistently above $200,000, the S-corp election conversation is overdue. The QBI deduction interacts with your W-2 wages paid, and S-corp owners can optimize the ratio of salary to distributions in ways that sole proprietors cannot. A 23% deduction amplifies those benefits.

Second, the Section 179 limit at $2.5 million means legitimate capital expenditure planning for the second half of 2026 deserves attention now, not in December. Equipment purchases, software licenses, and qualifying property placed in service before December 31 are on the table. Don't front-load purchases just for the deduction, but don't ignore the timing either.

Third, and this is the one people skip: document your qualified business income properly throughout the year. The QBI deduction applies to the net income from qualified trades or businesses, and if your bookkeeping is messy, you'll either miss deductions or create audit exposure. This is a mid-year books review conversation, not an April 15 scramble.

Finally, if you operate in a specified service trade, which includes law, consulting, financial services, and health, the phase-out thresholds apply to you more aggressively. At higher income levels, you may get little or no QBI deduction regardless of whether the rate is 20% or 23%. Know which bucket you're in before you plan around a deduction you might not fully capture.

The QBI landscape in mid-2026 is genuinely better than it was three years ago, and it could get better still. The permanence secured by the OBBBA removes the planning uncertainty that made this deduction frustrating to rely on. H.R. 8415 represents a real, if not guaranteed, upside. Get your structure right now, work with a qualified tax advisor on the specifics of your situation, and don't let the legislative noise distract you from the decisions that are fully in your control today.
