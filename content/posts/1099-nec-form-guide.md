If you pay a freelancer, [independent contractor](/independent-contractor-vs-employee/), or unincorporated service provider $2,000 or more in a calendar year, you're required to report that payment to the IRS using Form 1099-NEC. Miss the deadline or skip the form entirely and you're looking at penalties that stack up fast. That's the short version.

The longer version matters because the rules around who gets a 1099-NEC, what counts toward the $600 threshold, and how to actually file the form trip up a lot of small business owners every year. Let's get into it.

## Who Actually Gets One

The 1099-NEC covers non-employee compensation paid in the course of your business. That means freelancers, consultants, independent contractors, and sole proprietors you paid for services. It does not cover product purchases, rent paid to a property management company, or most payments made through a third-party payment processor like PayPal or Stripe (those get reported on a 1099-K, which is a separate form with its own thresholds).

A few categories people get wrong:

Attorneys always get a 1099-NEC if you paid them $600 or more for legal services, even if their firm is incorporated. That's one of the few corporate exceptions. Payments to an LLC depend on how the LLC is taxed: if it's a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietor, it needs a 1099-NEC. If it's taxed as an S-corp or C-corp, it generally doesn't.

You can verify these distinctions on [the IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed), where the instructions for Form 1099-NEC are available directly.

## The W-9 Is Your Cheat Code

Before you pay anyone who might require a 1099-NEC, get a completed Form W-9 from them. That form gives you their legal name, business name if different, tax classification, and taxpayer identification number (either a Social Security number or EIN). Without it, you're guessing at year-end, and guessing wrong means filing corrections.

The W-9 also tells you whether the contractor is incorporated. If box 3 on their W-9 shows "C Corporation" or "S Corporation," you typically don't need to file a 1099-NEC. It's right there on the form, which is why collecting it before the first payment is non-negotiable.

If a contractor refuses to provide a W-9, you're required to withhold 24% of payments for backup withholding and remit that to the IRS. That's a headache nobody wants.

## Deadlines and Penalties

The 1099-NEC deadline is strict: January 31 of the year following payment. That's the due date for both the recipient copy (the one you send the contractor) and the IRS copy. This is different from the 1099-MISC, which has different deadlines depending on what box you're reporting in. Many people mix these up.

Here's what the penalty structure looks like for 2026 (the IRS adjusts these for inflation periodically, so confirm current figures at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov)):

| Filing delay | Penalty per form |
| --- | --- |
| Up to 30 days late | $60 |
| 31 days late through August 1 | $120 |
| After August 1, or not filed | $310 |
| Intentional disregard | $630 |

If your business has gross receipts under $5 million, there are lower caps on annual penalties, but that doesn't make it a strategy. File on time.

## How to Actually File

You have two options: paper or electronic. If you're filing 10 or more information returns total (across all 1099 types and W-2s), the IRS requires electronic filing. Fewer than 10, paper is technically still allowed, but electronic is faster and gives you confirmation.

The IRS's own free system is called IRIS (Information Returns Intake System), available at IRS.gov. For most small businesses filing a handful of forms, it works fine. Third-party services like Track1099, Tax1099, or the 1099 module in QuickBooks Online are also widely used, typically charging a few dollars per form.

Step by step, here's the process:

1. Collect W-9s from all contractors before year-end. Chase any missing ones in December.
2. Pull total payments from your accounting records. Only count payments for services; exclude reimbursed expenses if you tracked them separately.
3. Identify which contractors hit the $2,000 threshold and which are exempt (corporations, etc.).
4. Prepare the 1099-NEC forms with the contractor's name, address, TIN, and total compensation in Box 1.
5. Send the recipient copy by January 31.
6. File with the IRS by January 31 (same date, unlike some other 1099 types).
7. Keep copies for at least four years.

The most common place things go wrong: contractor's TIN doesn't match IRS records. This triggers a CP2100 notice and you may need to initiate backup withholding. The fix is getting a corrected W-9 and filing a 1099 correction as soon as you catch it.

**Example:** A graphic design studio pays six contractors during the year. Two are sole proprietors paid $1,200 and $800 respectively. Two are single-member LLCs (taxed as sole proprietors) paid $700 and $400. One is an S-corp paid $3,000. One is an individual paid $450. For payments made in 2025, under the old $600 threshold, the studio files 1099-NECs for the $1,200 and $800 sole proprietors and the $700 LLC: three forms. The S-corp is exempt and the other two are below the threshold. If the same payments are made in 2026, none of the non-exempt payees reaches the new $2,000 threshold, so no 1099-NEC is required.

## State Filing Requirements

Federal isn't the whole story. Many states have their own 1099-NEC filing requirements, and they don't all follow federal rules or deadlines. Some states participate in the IRS Combined Federal/State Filing program, which means your federal filing satisfies your state requirement automatically. Others require a separate state submission. Check your state's department of revenue directly; there's no single federal resource that covers all 50 state requirements accurately.

The [U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA)](https://www.sba.gov/) has general guidance on contractor reporting obligations, but for state-specific deadlines and filing portals, go straight to your state's tax authority.
