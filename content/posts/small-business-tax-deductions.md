Small business owners leave thousands of dollars on the table every year, not because the deductions don't exist, but because nobody ever explained what qualifies, how to document it, or where to draw the line. ## What Makes an Expense Deductible in the First Place

Before you start making a list of everything you've ever bought for your business, understand the rule. The IRS requires that a deductible business expense be both **ordinary** and **necessary**. Ordinary means it's common and accepted in your industry. Necessary means it's appropriate and helpful for your business. You don't have to prove the expense was indispensable, just that it was genuinely connected to operating your business.

That's it. That's the core standard. Everything else in this article flows from it.

The flip side: personal expenses don't become deductible just because you paid for them with a business card. And mixed-use items, things you use for both business and personal purposes, need to be split. You can deduct the business-use percentage, not the whole thing. Keep that principle in your back pocket.

Here's where most of the real money is. These aren't obscure loopholes. They're legal, well-established deductions that millions of small businesses use every year.

**Self-employment tax deduction.** If you're a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, you pay both sides of Social Security and Medicare, which currently totals 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base. The good news: you can deduct half of that self-employment tax from your gross income. It doesn't reduce your self-employment tax, but it reduces your income tax. It's one of the first adjustments on Schedule 1 of your 1040.

**Home office deduction.** If you use part of your home regularly and exclusively for business, you can deduct it. There are two methods. The simplified method lets you deduct $5 per square foot of your office space, up to 300 square feet, for a maximum deduction of $1,500. The regular method calculates the actual percentage of your home used for business and applies it to real expenses like mortgage interest, rent, utilities, and insurance. The regular method is more paperwork but often produces a larger deduction.

**Vehicle expenses.** Same two-method situation. You can track actual vehicle expenses and deduct the business-use percentage, or you can use the IRS standard mileage rate, which changes annually, so always check the current rate at IRS.gov before filing. Keep a mileage log. Seriously. The IRS loves to challenge vehicle deductions, and "I drove a lot" doesn't hold up in an audit.

**Equipment and Section 179.** Under Section 179 of the tax code, you can deduct the full cost of qualifying equipment and software in the year you buy it, rather than depreciating it over several years. There are annual limits (which the IRS adjusts over time), but for most small businesses, Section 179 covers what you'd actually spend on a computer, machinery, or office furniture in a single year. Bonus depreciation rules can stack on top of this, though those rules have been phasing down, so confirm the current status with your CPA.

**Health insurance premiums.** If you're self-employed and not eligible for coverage through a spouse's employer plan, you can deduct 100% of health insurance premiums paid for yourself, your spouse, and your dependents. This is an above-the-line deduction, meaning you get it even if you don't itemize. It doesn't reduce self-employment tax, but it does reduce your income tax bill.

**Retirement plan contributions.** This one is underused, and it shouldn't be. Contributing to a SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), or SIMPLE IRA as a self-employed person lets you deduct those contributions from your taxable income. The SEP-IRA is one of the simplest, with contribution limits tied to a percentage of net self-employment income. The Solo 401(k) allows higher total contributions if you want to maximize your retirement savings. Your CPA can help you model which plan fits your income level.

## Business Expenses People Forget to Deduct

These are the ones that add up quietly and get missed just as quietly.

- **Business meals:** 50% deductible when there's a genuine business purpose. The client dinner, the lunch where you actually talked strategy, the meal during a business trip. Document who you met with and what you discussed.
- **Education and training:** Courses, books, conferences, and subscriptions that maintain or improve skills required in your current business. Note: costs related to entering a new profession don't qualify. A graphic designer buying a design course qualifies. That same designer buying a real estate licensing prep course does not.
- **Software and subscriptions:** Your accounting software, project management tools, industry publications, and cloud storage services all count if they're used for business.
- **Bank fees and merchant processing fees:** Every Stripe or Square fee, monthly account maintenance charge, and wire transfer fee is deductible.
- **Professional services:** Legal fees, accounting fees, consulting costs. If you paid someone to help you run or grow your business, that's deductible.
- **Marketing and advertising:** Website costs, paid ads, business cards, email marketing platforms. All of it.
- **Business insurance:** General liability, professional liability (E&O), and other business-related policies are deductible.

In my experience, a first-year business owner who starts tracking these carefully often discovers they've been over-paying taxes by a meaningful margin. Not because they were doing anything wrong, just because they weren't paying attention.

## A Step-by-Step System for Tracking Deductions Year-Round

The best tax outcome doesn't come from scrambling in April. It comes from a system you run all year. Here's a simple one that works.

**Step 1: Open a dedicated business bank account and credit card.** Never mix personal and business funds. This is the foundation. Every transaction on that account is either a business expense or it isn't, and sorting is much faster when you're not digging through personal Amazon orders.

**Step 2: Use accounting software from day one.** QuickBooks Self-Employed, Wave (free), or FreshBooks will categorize your transactions automatically once you set up the rules. You can link your bank and card directly. This also makes handing data off to a CPA dramatically easier.

**Step 3: Save receipts digitally.** Apps like Dext or even a simple Google Drive folder work fine. The IRS generally requires you to be able to produce documentation for deductions. A bank statement showing the charge helps, but for larger purchases, you want the actual receipt or invoice.

**Step 4: Log mileage in real time.** MileIQ or a basic spreadsheet with date, destination, business purpose, and miles. Don't try to reconstruct this from memory at year-end. You'll miss miles and your numbers won't hold up.

**Step 5: Do a monthly 30-minute review.** Sit down once a month and make sure all transactions are categorized. Flag anything you're unsure about. This prevents a 10-hour panic session every January.

**Step 6: Meet with a CPA at least once a year, ideally before the tax year ends.** There are moves you can make before December 31 that you can't make after. Buying equipment, making retirement contributions, timing income, prepaying deductible expenses. A year-end check-in is where that guidance pays off.

## Where People Get Into Trouble

Claiming deductions you're not entitled to isn't a gray area. It's tax fraud if done intentionally, or an expensive mistake if done carelessly. Here are the patterns that tend to create problems.

**Claiming 100% business use on a vehicle you also drive personally.** The IRS knows this is common, and they scrutinize it. If you claimed 100% but your car is also your family's weekend vehicle, that's a problem. Log honestly and deduct the honest percentage.

**The "hobby loss" trap.** If your business loses money three years out of five, the IRS may reclassify it as a hobby, which eliminates your ability to deduct losses against other income. This isn't just a technicality. It can result in back taxes and penalties. The key is demonstrating that you operate the activity like a business: separate accounts, business plans, marketing activity, and a genuine attempt at profitability.

**Taking the home office deduction incorrectly.** "Regular and exclusive use" is the standard. A guest bedroom that also has your desk doesn't qualify. A room used only for work does. Don't claim it if you can't defend the exclusive-use test.

**Missing the QBI deduction.** The Qualified Business Income deduction, created by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, allows many pass-through business owners to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. It has income thresholds and profession-based restrictions that make it complicated. Many small business owners either miss it entirely or assume they don't qualify without checking. Your CPA needs to run the numbers on this one specifically.

## FAQ

### Can I deduct startup costs before my business officially opens?

Yes, with limits. The IRS allows you to deduct up to $5,000 in startup costs and $5,000 in organizational costs in your first year of business. Costs above those amounts must be amortized over 180 months. Qualifying startup expenses include market research, advertising before launch, and employee training costs incurred before opening. Keep clear records of when you incurred these costs and what they were for. Talk to your CPA about how to structure this if your startup costs were significant.

### Is my business lunch with a friend deductible if we talked about business?

Probably not. The IRS requires that business meals have a "bona fide business purpose" and that business be actually discussed. Catching up with a friend who happens to also be in business doesn't meet the standard. The meal needs to directly relate to your business: a client meeting, a vendor discussion, a sales call. Document who you met with and what specifically was discussed.

### What if I use my personal phone for business? Can I deduct it?

Yes, but only the business-use portion. If you use your phone 60% for business and 40% personally, you can deduct 60% of the bill. This requires a reasonable, honest estimate. The easiest approach is to review a month or two of usage and apply a consistent percentage going forward.

### Do I need to file a separate tax return for an LLC?

It depends on how your LLC is structured. A single-member LLC is treated as a "disregarded entity" by default, which means its income and expenses flow to your personal return on Schedule C. A multi-member LLC files a partnership return (Form 1065). An LLC taxed as an S-corp files Form 1120-S. The structure matters for both tax treatment and self-employment tax, and it's worth reviewing with a CPA if you haven't already.

### How long should I keep tax records?

The IRS generally has three years from your filing date to audit your return, assuming you've reported your income fully. That window extends to six years if the IRS believes you've underreported income by more than 25%, and there's no time limit in cases of fraud. A practical rule: keep all business tax records and supporting documentation for at least seven years. Digital storage makes this easy. There's no reason to throw anything away.
