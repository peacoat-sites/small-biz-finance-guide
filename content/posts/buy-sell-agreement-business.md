Most business partnerships end. Not because people are bad partners, but because life happens: someone gets sick, someone wants out, someone dies, a marriage falls apart and a spouse suddenly owns a third of your company. If you don't have a written agreement that spells out exactly what happens in those moments, you're leaving one of the biggest financial decisions of your life to chance, or worse, to a judge who has never met you.

That's where a buy-sell agreement comes in. And if you're reading this, you might be wondering whether you actually need one, how much it costs, and whether it's the kind of thing you can just Google your way through. I'll give you honest answers to all three.

I've sat across from enough [business owners](/the-new-1099-thresholds-small-business-owners-must-know-in-2026/) after a crisis hit to know that the ones who had a solid buy-sell in place walked out okay. The ones who didn't? Some of them are still in litigation years later.

## What a Buy-Sell Agreement Actually Does

Think of it as a prenuptial agreement for your business. It's a legally binding contract between co-owners that defines the rules for buying out an owner's interest under specific triggering events. Those events usually include death, disability, retirement, divorce, bankruptcy, or a voluntary exit.

Here's what I tell people when they first hear the term: it's not about distrust. It's about clarity. The agreement doesn't assume anything bad will happen. It just says, "if it does, here's what we agreed to ahead of time, when we were all reasonable and the stakes weren't personal yet."

The structure has two core components. First, the triggering events (what situations activate the agreement). Second, the valuation and funding mechanism (how much gets paid, and where the money comes from). Both matter equally, and most disputes I've seen trace back to one of those two being vague or missing entirely.

A buy-sell agreement can also restrict who can own shares. If your partner wants to bring in a new investor or give equity to a family member, the agreement can require consent from other owners first. That's not a minor thing. I've seen businesses completely derailed by an ownership transfer that nobody saw coming.

Not all buy-sell agreements are built the same way. The structure you choose affects who buys the departing owner's interest, how the money flows, and what the tax implications look like.

**Cross-purchase agreements:** Each individual owner agrees to buy out the departing owner's share. If there are three equal partners and one dies, the two remaining owners each buy half of the departing owner's interest directly. Simple in concept, complicated with more than three or four owners because each owner needs separate life insurance on every other owner. With five partners that's 20 policies. That math gets unwieldy fast.

**Entity-purchase (or redemption) agreements:** The business itself buys back the departing owner's interest. One set of policies, owned by the company. Cleaner administratively, but the tax treatment is different, especially for C-corps where there can be a dividend issue on proceeds. Consult a CPA before locking in this structure.

**Hybrid (or wait-and-see) agreements:** The business gets the first right of refusal to buy the interest. If it can't or won't, the remaining owners step in. This is the most flexible, which is probably why it's become the most popular structure for small businesses with two to four owners.

## The Valuation Problem (This Is Where Things Get Messy)

I'll be direct: valuation is the part most business owners handle worst. And it's the part that matters most when the agreement actually gets triggered.

You have a few options for how to set the buyout price:

| Method | How It Works | Best For | Common Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed price | Owners agree on a dollar amount upfront | Simple, early-stage businesses | Goes stale fast; needs regular updates |
| Formula-based | Buyout price is calculated (e.g., 3x EBITDA) | Businesses with predictable cash flow | Formula may not reflect market reality |
| Independent appraisal | Third-party appraiser values the business at time of trigger | Most situations | Expensive ($3,000-$15,000+); can be disputed |
| Right of first refusal | Departing owner solicits outside offers; co-owners can match | Businesses with clear market comps | Creates delays; tensions run high |

The fixed-price method is almost always a mistake unless you build in a mandatory annual review, and even then, most owners don't actually do the review. I've seen a buy-sell agreement written in 2015 with a $400,000 valuation that triggered in 2023 when the business was worth close to $2.1 million. The surviving partner had to fight in court for months to get a fair outcome. The legal fees alone were north of $80,000.

Formula-based valuation is my preferred starting point for [most small businesses](/the-obbba-childcare-credit-most-small-businesses-are-ignoring/), with a backstop appraisal clause if the parties can't agree. Something like "3x trailing 12-month EBITDA, or independent appraisal if either party disputes the calculation within 30 days." It creates a clear default without locking you into a number that becomes wrong.

## How the Buyout Gets Funded

A valuation method tells you the price. Funding tells you where the cash comes from. This is the piece that people often forget entirely, which is a problem because a buy-sell agreement without a funding mechanism is essentially a promise you might not be able to keep.

The three realistic options:

**Life insurance:** Covers the death trigger specifically, and it's the cleanest solution. The policy pays out, the surviving owners use the proceeds to buy the deceased's interest. The family gets cash, the business stays intact, nobody has to go to a bank under duress. As of July 2026, a healthy 42-year-old business owner can get $1 million in 20-year term coverage for roughly $600 to $900 per year, which is genuinely affordable for the protection it provides.

**Installment payments:** The business pays out over time, often three to five years. This works when a buyout is planned (retirement), but it puts real strain on cash flow and the departing owner takes on credit risk against the business they just left.

**Sinking fund:** The company sets aside cash over time specifically for a future buyout. Sounds prudent. Rarely actually happens with small businesses. I'd call this the theoretical option.

In my experience, a combination of life insurance (for death) and an installment structure (for everything else) is what actually works for most businesses with one to five owners. The [SCORE mentorship network](https://www.score.org/) has free templates and advisors who can help you think through the mechanics if you want a second set of eyes before you spend money on an attorney.

## What It Costs, and What Skipping It Costs

Attorney fees for a buy-sell agreement typically run $1,500 to $3,500 for a straightforward two-owner business, and $5,000 to $12,000 or more for complex multi-owner structures with custom valuation provisions. A [business valuation](/business-valuation-methods/) firm may charge an additional $3,000 to $15,000 if you want a formal appraisal baked into the document.

That sounds like money. But the first time I saw a business owner go through a contested ownership dispute without a buy-sell agreement, the legal fees hit $140,000 over 18 months, and the business nearly folded from the distraction alone. The cost comparison isn't close.

One practical note: you can find decent buy-sell agreement templates online for $50 to $200, but I'd treat those as a starting point for a conversation with an attorney, not as a finished document. State laws on business entities vary significantly, and a generic template won't catch the specific provisions your situation needs. The [IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is also worth checking because the tax treatment of buyout proceeds can vary meaningfully based on how the agreement is structured.

If you want a deeper foundation on business agreements and ownership structures, *The E-Myth Revisited* by Michael Gerber (available on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/E-Myth-Revisited-Small-Businesses-About/dp/0887307280)) doesn't cover buy-sell agreements directly, but it reframes how you think about your business as a system that operates independently of any one person, which is exactly the mindset that makes you actually go draft the agreement instead of putting it off. ## What a Triggered Agreement Looks Like in Practice

Three worked examples, because the abstract version doesn't stick:

**Scenario 1:** Two restaurant co-owners, 50/50, no buy-sell. One owner dies in 2023. Surviving spouse inherits 50% ownership and has zero interest in running a restaurant. She wants cash. Owner wants the business. No agreement, no funding, no roadmap. → Litigation for 14 months, business operations disrupted, $97,000 in legal fees split between the estate and the surviving owner, final settlement significantly below fair market value for both parties.

**Scenario 2:** Same setup, but they had a cross-purchase agreement funded by $800,000 in term life insurance on each owner. One owner dies. → Surviving owner receives $800,000 in insurance proceeds, pays the estate per the agreed formula, business continues without disruption. Total time from death to completed transfer: 47 days.

**Scenario 3:** Three-owner professional services firm, one partner wants to retire at 60. They had a hybrid agreement with a 4x EBITDA formula and five-year installment payout. → Retiring partner receives $1.2 million over five years, remaining partners absorb the equity stake proportionally, business cash flow handles the payments without outside financing. Exactly what the agreement was designed for.
