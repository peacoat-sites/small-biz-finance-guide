Selling online is no longer a growth strategy for [small businesses](/the-section-45f-childcare-credit-small-businesses-are-missing/). For many, it's the difference between staying open and closing. The good news is that getting an e-commerce operation off the ground has never been more accessible or more affordable. The hard part is making smart choices up front, because a bad platform decision or a sloppy checkout setup will cost you far more than whatever you saved by rushing.

The market today has real options at every price point. Shopify, WooCommerce, BigCommerce, Wix eCommerce, Squarespace Commerce, and Square Online each serve different types of businesses. None is universally best. The right one depends on your product type, your technical comfort level, your expected order volume, and whether you're selling physical goods, digital products, services, or some mix of all three.

## Choosing a Platform (And What They Don't Tell You)

The honest comparison most people skip is total cost, not just the monthly subscription. Shopify's base plan runs around $39/month but adds a transaction fee if you don't use Shopify Payments. WooCommerce is [free software](/wave-accounting-free-software/) but you'll pay for hosting, an SSL certificate, and likely several paid plugins before you're done. BigCommerce charges no transaction fees but limits your annual sales volume on lower-tier plans. These distinctions matter a lot at scale.

| Platform | Starting Monthly Cost | Transaction Fee (own processor) | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shopify Basic | ~$39 | 2% (waived with Shopify Payments) | Product-based businesses, beginners |
| WooCommerce | ~$0 software + $5–25 hosting | None | WordPress users, customization needs |
| BigCommerce Standard | ~$39 | None | Growing product catalogs |
| Squarespace Commerce Basic | ~$28 | None | Service businesses, visual brands |
| Wix eCommerce | ~$17 | None | Simple stores, low volume |
| Square Online | Free tier available | None (Square payment fees apply) | Brick-and-mortar adding online |

*Note: Prices change. Verify current pricing directly with each provider before committing.*

A common mistake is treating the monthly fee as the real cost. It isn't. Add up [payment processing](/ach-payment-processing/), any app subscriptions you'll need (email marketing, reviews, shipping automation), and the transaction fees above, and the spread between platforms can easily be $100–$300/month at modest sales volumes.

If you're selling fewer than 50 orders a month, almost any platform will do. Once you're regularly above 200 orders a month, you'll want to look hard at fulfillment integrations and whether your platform talks cleanly to your accounting software (QuickBooks, Xero, FreshBooks). That sync matters more than most people expect.

## The Checkout Problem

Roughly 70% of online shopping carts get abandoned before purchase, according to data the Baymard Institute has tracked consistently for years. That's not a typo. Most of those abandonments are preventable: forced account creation, surprise shipping costs at checkout, too many form fields, and slow page loads are the top culprits.

Fix the basics first. Enable guest checkout. Show shipping costs before the final step (or offer a shipping calculator early in the cart). Keep your checkout to as few fields as possible. And set up abandoned cart emails, which most major platforms include natively or via a simple integration. A three-email sequence (sent at 1 hour, 24 hours, and 72 hours after abandonment) is a standard setup and genuinely recovers sales.

The payment processor choice also affects conversion. Offering PayPal alongside a card processor typically lifts conversions, especially on mobile. Stripe and Square both have solid small business pricing. Check the [IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) if you're new to collecting payments online; there are reporting obligations that kick in at certain thresholds and you want to know about them before tax season.

## Shipping: Where Margins Actually Die

Pricing your shipping wrong is one of the fastest ways to destroy profitability. Free shipping is powerful for conversion, but it isn't free for you. If your average order is $35 and your average shipping cost is $7, that's a 20% margin hit before you've accounted for packaging.

**Example:** Assume you sell 300 orders a month at $40 average order value. Gross revenue is $12,000. If you offer free shipping and your average shipping cost is $6.50, you're absorbing $1,950 in shipping per month. At $22,800 annually, that's a real number. Build it into your product pricing if you're going to offer it, don't just bolt it on and hope.

USPS, UPS, and FedEx all offer discounted rates for small businesses. Shopify Shipping, ShipStation, and Pirateship are tools that give you access to commercial rates even at low volume. Pirateship in particular is worth a look for businesses under 500 shipments a month; it passes through USPS Commercial Plus pricing with no monthly fee.

## Sales Tax: Don't Ignore This

Since the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in *South Dakota v. Wayfair*, states can require online sellers to collect sales tax even without a physical presence there. This is called economic nexus and it now exists in nearly every state, typically triggered at $100,000 in annual sales or 200 transactions in that state. If you're selling across the country and haven't reviewed your nexus obligations, this is overdue.

TaxJar and Avalara both automate sales tax calculation and filing for small e-commerce businesses. Neither is free, but the cost is small relative to a state audit. The [U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/) has a business guide section that covers state compliance basics. For anything complex, talk to a CPA who works with e-commerce companies specifically.

## Marketing Your Store

You can build a beautiful store and hear nothing but crickets. Getting traffic is a separate job from building the site, and it's one a lot of new sellers underestimate.

Organic search (SEO) takes time, usually six months or more before you see meaningful results. Paid search (Google Shopping ads) can work immediately but requires a budget and some knowledge to avoid wasting money. Email marketing returns consistently high ROI relative to cost and should be a priority from day one, even if your list starts at 50 people.

Social commerce (selling directly through Instagram or TikTok Shop) is a real channel now, not an experiment. TikTok Shop in particular has driven meaningful volume for small product brands, especially in beauty, apparel, and home goods. It won't work for every business, but if your product photographs or demos well, it's worth testing a small budget before dismissing it.

For anyone building a longer-term content strategy, *They Ask, You Answer* by Marcus Sheridan (available on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/They-Ask-You-Answer-Revolutionary/dp/1119312973?tag=contentportfo-20)) is one of the more practical books on using content to drive inbound traffic.
