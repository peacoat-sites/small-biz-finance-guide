Most people ask this question when they're about 48 hours into setting up their LLC and suddenly realize they don't know what an EIN actually is, let alone whether they need one. You've got the state filing done, or maybe you're still waiting on it, and now someone (a bank, an accountant, a YouTube video) has thrown this acronym at you. That's a completely normal place to be. Let me walk you through this the way I'd explain it sitting across from you.

An EIN is an Employer Identification Number, issued free by the IRS. Think of it as a Social Security number for your business. Nine digits, formatted like 12-3456789. The IRS uses it to track your business's tax filings. Banks use it to [open business](/how-to-open-a-business-bank-account-online-for-startup/) accounts. Some vendors require it before they'll pay you. It's not a state-level thing; it's federal, and it's permanent once you get it.

Here's the short version before we go deeper: you probably need one, even if you think you don't.

## When the IRS Says You're Required to Have One

The IRS is pretty specific here. Your LLC is legally required to have an EIN if any of the following apply: you have employees (even one), you have more than one member, you withhold taxes on non-resident aliens, or you file employment, excise, or alcohol/tobacco/firearms tax returns. That last one trips people up sometimes.

For a single-member LLC with no employees and no unusual tax situations, the IRS technically lets you use your own SSN on federal tax filings. Your LLC is treated as a "disregarded entity" by default, meaning it's invisible to the IRS for tax purposes. The income flows straight to your personal return on Schedule C. No [separate business](/how-to-separate-business-personal-finances/) tax return, no separate business tax ID required.

On paper, that's fine. In practice, it creates problems.

I used to tell clients this was optional for solo LLCs. I was wrong, and I figured that out the hard way after a client of mine had her business account application rejected three times because she'd been giving vendors her SSN, which flagged the account setup as a potential identity risk. Small moment. Expensive lesson.

Here's what actually happens in the real world. Nearly every bank, credit union, and business payment platform will ask for an EIN when you try to open a business account. Chase, Bank of America, Mercury, Relay, even your local community bank. They want that number. Some will technically accept an SSN for a sole proprietor, but an LLC is not a [sole proprietorship](/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship-taxes/), even a single-member one. The bank's compliance department often treats them differently, and without an EIN, you can get stuck in a documentation loop that wastes weeks.

There's also the identity exposure angle. Every time you hand your SSN to a vendor, a contractor, or a client who needs to issue you a 1099, you're spreading around the most sensitive number you have. An EIN lets you compartmentalize. Give the EIN to business contacts, keep the SSN for tax filings where it belongs.

And the privacy argument matters less than the practical one: if you ever want to hire someone, bring in a partner, or change your tax classification to S-corp (which many LLC owners do once they're profitable enough, often around $50,000-$60,000 in net income annually), you'll need an EIN anyway. Might as well get it now when there's no pressure.

## The Scenarios That Determine Your Answer

Different LLC types, different situations. Here's how I typically map it out for clients:

| LLC Type | Employees? | EIN Required? | My Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-member, no employees | No | Not legally required | Get one anyway |
| Single-member, 1+ employees | Yes | Yes, required | Get one immediately |
| Multi-member LLC (partnership tax treatment) | No | Yes, required | Get one before filing |
| LLC taxed as S-corp | Varies | Yes, required | Essential from day one |
| LLC taxed as C-corp | Varies | Yes, required | Essential from day one |
| Single-member, no employees, no bank account needed | No | Not legally required | Your call, but still worth doing |

The only situation where I genuinely wouldn't stress about it is someone running a very small, cash-only operation with no employees, no business bank account, and no plans to grow. And honestly? That describes almost nobody who's gone through the effort of forming an LLC.

## How to Actually Get One (It's Easier Than You Think)

This is where I see people waste money unnecessarily. Third-party services charge anywhere from $49 to $299 to file for your EIN. I've seen LegalZoom charge $79 for this. Do not pay that. The IRS issues EINs for free, directly, at irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online.

As of August 2026, the online application is available Monday through Friday, 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. Eastern time. It takes about 10 minutes, you answer a handful of questions about your LLC, and your EIN is issued on the screen at the end. Print it or save it as a PDF immediately; the IRS doesn't email it to you and won't let you retrieve it the same way again. You'll eventually receive a CP 575 confirmation letter in the mail (usually within four weeks), which is the official document some institutions want to see.

One thing that catches people: the online application requires that the "responsible party" have an SSN or ITIN. That's the person legally accountable for the entity. For most single-member LLCs, that's obviously you. If your LLC is foreign-owned or the responsible party doesn't have a U.S. tax ID, you'll have to file Form SS-4 by fax or mail instead, which currently takes several weeks.

## What the EIN Doesn't Do

You might be wondering, once I have an EIN, do I need anything else? Short answer: yes, other things, but the EIN is not a business license, a state tax registration, a sales tax permit, or proof that your LLC is in good standing. Those are separate and vary by state. The EIN is purely a federal tax ID. It doesn't replace the need to register with your state's revenue department if you're selling taxable goods or services.

The SCORE mentorship program has solid free resources on what registrations your state may require beyond the EIN, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources cover what financial institutions typically need when you're setting up your first business account. Worth bookmarking both.

Some people also assume the EIN protects their personal liability. It doesn't. The LLC structure itself does that (imperfectly, and only if you maintain separation between personal and business finances). The EIN is a tax administration tool. Important one, but not a legal shield.
