15.3%. That's the self-employment tax rate, and it hits you on every dollar of net profit before you've paid a cent of income tax. Most new freelancers don't see it coming. I've watched otherwise sharp business owners stare at their first quarterly estimate like it's written in a foreign language, because nobody warned them that going solo means you're now paying both sides of Social Security and Medicare yourself.

Let me back up and make this concrete.

When you work for someone else, your employer pays half of your FICA taxes (7.65%) and withholds the other half from your paycheck. The moment you're self-employed, you become both employer and employee. The IRS collects all 15.3% from you. According to IRS data, approximately 16 million Americans filed Schedule SE in recent years, and the number keeps climbing as independent contracting grows. A lot of those people were surprised by the math. You don't have to be.

## What Schedule SE Actually Is

Schedule SE is the one-page form that calculates your self-employment tax. It doesn't live inside your income tax return exactly, it feeds into it. You file it with your Form 1040, and the resulting SE tax flows to Schedule 2 as an additional tax on top of whatever income tax you owe.

The form comes in two versions: a short form and a long form, though as of this year the IRS has consolidated things somewhat. Most people with straightforward self-employment income use the regular calculation. If you had wages from a job AND self-employment income, the long form accounts for wages you already paid Social Security tax on, which can reduce your SE tax. Worth knowing.

One thing I tell every client when they first file: the SE tax is based on 92.35% of your net self-employment income, not 100%. That percentage exists because employees get to exclude the "employer half" before calculating their FICA. The IRS applies that same logic here. So if your net profit is $80,000, you're calculating SE tax on $73,880 (that's $80,000 x 0.9235). At 15.3%, that's $11,304 in SE tax. Not nothing.

The 15.3% rate isn't monolithic. It has two pieces, and the Social Security piece has a ceiling.

| Component | Rate | 2026 Wage Base Cap | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social Security | 12.4% | $176,100 | Only applies up to the cap |
| Medicare | 2.9% | No cap | Applies to all net SE income |
| Additional Medicare Tax | 0.9% | Above $200,000 (single) | Applies to earnings over threshold |
| **Total (under cap)** | **15.3%** | | Most self-employed people pay this |
| **Total (over $200K, single)** | **16.2%** | | After Additional Medicare kicks in |

The Social Security wage base for 2026 is $176,100, which the IRS adjusts annually for inflation. If you earn more than that from self-employment (or a combination of wages and self-employment), you stop paying the 12.4% Social Security portion on amounts above the cap, and only pay the 2.9% Medicare piece. High earners often forget that the Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% kicks in above $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for married filing jointly, it's not built into SE tax, but it does show up on your return.

## The Deduction Most People Miss

Here's where I've seen people leave real money on the table. You can deduct half of your SE tax when calculating your adjusted gross income (AGI). Not as a business expense on Schedule C, on Schedule 1, Line 15, as an "above the line" deduction.

I thought for the first year I was self-employed that this was some obscure workaround. It's not. It's written directly into the tax code (IRC Section 164(f)), and it's the IRS acknowledging that employers get to deduct their share of FICA as a business expense, so the self-employed should get the same treatment on their half.

Concrete example: Net SE income of $80,000 → SE tax of $11,304 → deductible half is $5,652 → your AGI drops by $5,652. If you're in the 22% bracket, that's about $1,243 back in your pocket on income tax. Not the biggest number in the world, but it's yours, and you should take it.

## Quarterly Estimated Payments

Schedule SE tells you what you owe at year-end. But the IRS expects you to pay throughout the year. That's what estimated tax payments are for.

The due dates for 2026 are: April 15, June 16, September 15, and January 15, 2027. Miss these, and you can owe an underpayment penalty even if you pay the full amount by tax day. The current penalty rate is based on the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, which as of mid-2026 is meaningful enough to bother avoiding.

The safest method: calculate 100% of last year's total tax liability and divide by four. Pay that, and you're covered under the IRS safe harbor rule, regardless of how much you actually earn this year. If your income grew significantly, this can leave you underpaying, but you won't face a penalty. (If your prior year AGI was above $150,000, the safe harbor is 110% of last year's tax, not 100%.)

A reader named Dominique emailed me last spring after getting a $340 underpayment penalty she didn't see coming. She'd paid a lump sum on April 15 for the prior year's taxes but hadn't made any quarterly payments for the current year. She was technically current on one year but behind on the next. The IRS sees these as completely separate obligations. Easy mistake. Expensive one.

## Three Real Scenarios

**Scenario 1:** Freelance graphic designer, single, $45,000 net profit on Schedule C → SE tax calculated on $41,557 (92.35%) → SE tax owed: $6,358 → AGI deduction for half SE tax: $3,179 → reduces federal income tax by approximately $700 in the 22% bracket.

**Scenario 2:** Sole proprietor with a day job, $120,000 wages from employer plus $30,000 net SE income → Social Security wages already maxed out at the employer level → long Schedule SE applies → only Medicare tax (2.9%) on the SE income → SE tax drops to roughly $803 instead of $4,239. This is the scenario where the long form of Schedule SE actually saves you money, and a lot of people file the short form and overpay.

**Scenario 3:** High-earning consultant, single, $210,000 net SE income → Social Security applies only up to $176,100 → Medicare at 2.9% on all $193,936 (92.35%) → Additional Medicare 0.9% on amounts above $200,000 → total SE tax approximately $28,100, plus Additional Medicare. The math gets complicated fast above six figures, and this is where I'd say: stop trying to DIY it and pay a CPA for one hour of their time.

## What the Software Gets Wrong

Most tax software handles Schedule SE fine for straightforward situations. Where I've seen errors: when someone has both W-2 income and self-employment income and doesn't realize the software needs both numbers to calculate the reduced SE tax correctly. TurboTax and H&R Block both have this functionality, but you have to enter your W-2 first. The order matters. If you enter SE income in isolation and plan to add the W-2 later, the SE tax estimate in the summary screen will be wrong.

If you want to understand the underlying mechanics before sitting down with software, the IRS's own Publication 334 (Tax Guide for Small Business) is genuinely readable. And SCORE's [mentorship resources](https://www.score.org/) include tax prep guides that are free and not trying to sell you anything.

For deeper reading, Mike Piper's *Taxes Made Simple* (available [on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Taxes-Made-Simple-Income-Explained/dp/0997946539), and yes the site may earn a commission) explains Schedule SE in plain English with worked examples. It's not glamorous, but it's accurate and it's short.
