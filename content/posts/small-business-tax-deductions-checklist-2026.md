Most small business owners I work with are leaving real money on the table every single year. Not because they're careless. Because nobody ever handed them a complete picture of what's actually deductible, and the IRS certainly isn't going to volunteer that information.

I'll be honest: I spent a good chunk of time going back through the current tax code, cross-referencing with the [IRS small business tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed), and thinking about what's changed heading into 2026. What surprised me was how many of the deductions that trip people up aren't obscure loopholes. They're ordinary expenses that business owners either forget to track, miscategorize, or talk themselves out of claiming because they're not sure they qualify.

## The "Everyone Knows These" Deductions (But Still Gets Wrong)

Home office. Vehicle. Phone. These are the ones every article mentions, which is partly why people get them wrong.

The home office deduction is misunderstood in a specific way: most people think it requires a dedicated room. It doesn't require a room. It requires a space used *regularly and exclusively* for business. A corner of your bedroom with a desk technically qualifies if you use it only for work. What doesn't qualify is your kitchen table where you also eat breakfast. The IRS is serious about the "exclusive use" requirement, so don't stretch it.

You have two calculation methods. The simplified method gives you $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet, so a maximum deduction of $1,500. The regular method calculates your actual home expenses (mortgage interest or rent, utilities, insurance, repairs) and applies the percentage of your home used for business. If your home office is 200 square feet in a 2,000 square foot house, that's 10% of every qualifying home expense. The regular method almost always produces a larger deduction. Almost always worth the extra math.

Vehicle expenses. Same deal. Two methods: standard mileage rate (67 cents per mile for 2024, check for the 2025/2026 rate when it's published) or actual expenses (gas, insurance, maintenance, depreciation). You have to pick one method and stick with it for the life of the vehicle. Most sole proprietors do better with standard mileage on a typical sedan. If you drive a heavy SUV or work truck heavily for business, run both calculations.

The part people mess up: they track business miles but forget about the odometer reading on January 1st. You need total annual miles to calculate your business-use percentage. Set a phone reminder right now.

Phone and internet. If you use your personal phone and home internet for business (and who doesn't), you can deduct the business-use percentage. Most self-employed people I've worked with reasonably allocate somewhere between 50% and 80% for business, depending on their actual use. Be honest about this. Claiming 100% on a personal cell phone is a fast way to create problems if you're ever audited.

This one consistently comes up when I'm reviewing a client's books for the first time. They've been paying for services that are clearly deductible and just... not tracking them as business expenses.

Your bookkeeper or accountant fees? Deductible. Legal fees for business matters? Deductible. The fee you paid a consultant to help you write a proposal? Deductible. What about tax preparation fees? The portion attributable to your business return (Schedule C, Form 1065, corporate return) is deductible as a business expense. The personal portion isn't.

Software subscriptions are a big one. QuickBooks at roughly $30-$100/month depending on the plan. Your project management tool. The scheduling software. Zoom. Adobe Creative Cloud if you use it for work. Every recurring SaaS subscription that touches your business operations is deductible. Make a list of every card charge that says "subscription" in your bank feed and sort out which ones are business. Most people find two or three they forgot about entirely.

Marketing and advertising: all of it. Website hosting, domain fees, Google Ads spend, Meta ad spend, your Mailchimp subscription, the graphic designer you hired on Fiverr, the branded swag you ordered for a trade show. If it was spent with the intention of generating revenue, it goes here.

## Depreciation, Section 179, and Bonus Depreciation: The Part Nobody Explains Well

Here's where I lose a lot of business owners, and I get why.

When you buy a piece of equipment, a computer, machinery, furniture for your office, the IRS generally wants you to spread that deduction over multiple years (called depreciation). A $3,000 laptop might be deducted over 5 years under normal depreciation rules, not all at once.

Section 179 is the exception. It lets you deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment and software in the year you buy it, rather than spreading it out. For 2024 the limit was $1,160,000 (yes, million -- this is written for businesses of all sizes), with a phase-out starting at $2,890,000 in total purchases. Those numbers will adjust for inflation in 2026 -- your CPA can confirm the current figure when you're actually filing.

Bonus depreciation is a related but separate concept. It was 100% for several years, meaning you could write off the entire cost of eligible property in year one. It dropped to 60% for 2024, and under current law it's scheduled to continue phasing down. By 2026 it may be at 40% unless Congress acts. The research here is genuinely uncertain because tax law is subject to change, and there's been discussion about legislation that could extend or modify these provisions.

The practical takeaway: if you bought or plan to buy equipment, vehicles, or software for your business, talk to your accountant *before* year-end about whether Section 179 or bonus depreciation applies. Timing a major purchase by December 31st versus January 1st can be a significant difference.

## The Deductions People Talk Themselves Out Of

Meals. You can deduct 50% of business meals where you're discussing business with a client, employee, or prospect. The meals have to have a legitimate business purpose, and you need to document who you were with and what you discussed. The days of the three-martini lunch being fully deductible are long gone, but 50% is still real money if you're regularly entertaining clients or having working meals with your team. Keep the receipts. Write a note on the back or in your accounting software.

Business travel. Flights, hotels, rental cars, 50% of meals while traveling for business -- all deductible. What surprised me when I dug into this is how often small business owners attend conferences or travel to meet vendors and don't fully capture the deductions. The conference registration fee is deductible. The Uber to the airport is deductible. The checked bag fee. People pick up the hotel bill and get it, but miss the $12 airport sandwich and the $38 Lyft ride.

Education and training. If you paid for a course, workshop, certification, or even books that maintain or improve skills required in your current business, that's deductible. Note the word "current" -- education that helps you enter a new profession doesn't qualify. But a marketing course for a marketing consultant, an accounting software certification for a bookkeeper, a leadership seminar for a business owner? All of it.

Retirement contributions. This is the one I am most passionate about because it's both a deduction and a wealth-building tool. If you're self-employed and contributing to a SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or Solo 401(k), those contributions are deductible. A SEP-IRA lets you contribute up to 25% of net self-employment income (up to a maximum that adjusts annually). A Solo 401(k) has even higher combined limits. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has some plain-language explainers on retirement options for self-employed people if you want a starting point before you talk to an advisor.

Health insurance premiums. If you're self-employed and not eligible for coverage through a spouse's employer, you can deduct 100% of health insurance premiums for yourself, your spouse, and your dependents. Not as a business expense on your Schedule C -- it comes off your adjusted gross income on Form 1040. The distinction matters for self-employment tax calculations. Ask your CPA how this flows through on your specific return.

## How to Actually Use This Before Year-End

Here's what I'd actually do if I were sitting across from you right now. Pull up your bank statements and credit card statements from January through today. Go line by line. For every expense, ask: was this ordinary and necessary for my business? That's the IRS's actual standard -- ordinary (normal for your type of business) and necessary (appropriate and helpful).

Create simple categories: office/home, vehicle, marketing, professional services, equipment, travel, meals, education, insurance, retirement. Drop each deductible expense into a bucket. Most modern accounting software, QuickBooks, Wave (free), FreshBooks, will do a lot of this automatically if you've been categorizing as you go. If you haven't been categorizing as you go, now is the time to catch up.

Before December 31st, think about what you might want to accelerate. Software subscriptions you were going to buy in January? Buy in December. Equipment you need? Consider buying before year-end if Section 179 makes sense in your situation. Charitable contributions? Those are personal deductions, not business, but they're real.

## FAQ

### Can I deduct my startup costs if I'm in my first year of business?

Yes, but with limits. The IRS allows you to deduct up to $5,000 in startup costs and $5,000 in organizational costs in your first year, with the remainder amortized over 15 years. This applies only if total startup costs are under $50,000 -- after that, the $5,000 first-year deduction phases out.

### Is my business insurance premium deductible?

Yes. Premiums for general liability insurance, professional liability (errors and omissions), business property insurance, and workers' compensation are all deductible as ordinary business expenses. This is one of the cleanest, most straightforward deductions on the list.

### What records do I actually need to keep, and for how long?

The IRS generally recommends keeping supporting documents (receipts, invoices, bank statements, mileage logs) for at least three years from the date you filed, since that's the standard audit window. For employment tax records, keep them four years. Some CPAs recommend keeping everything for seven years as a conservative buffer.

### Can I deduct gifts I give to clients?

Yes, but only up to $25 per recipient per year. This limit has been the same since 1954 and has never been adjusted for inflation, which tells you something about how Congress thinks about small business owners. Incidental costs like gift wrapping don't count toward the $25 limit.

### What's the difference between a deduction and a tax credit?

A deduction reduces your taxable income. A credit reduces your actual tax bill dollar for dollar. A $1,000 deduction saves you $220 if you're in the 22% bracket. A $1,000 credit saves you $1,000 regardless of bracket. Credits are more valuable, but there are far fewer of them. The Small Business Health Care Tax Credit is one worth knowing about if you have employees and provide health coverage.
