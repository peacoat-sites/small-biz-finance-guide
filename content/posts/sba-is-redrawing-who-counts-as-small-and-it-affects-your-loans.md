The SBA quietly dropped a 400-page proposed rule in August 2026, and it could change whether your business qualifies as "small" under federal definitions. That matters more than most owners realize, because the word "small" is not just a label. It's a gatekeeper. It determines whether you can apply for SBA 7(a) or 504 loans, bid on federal contracts reserved for small businesses, or access dozens of other government programs. If you've ever been told your company is too big to qualify, this rule is worth your full attention.

Published on August 20, 2026 in the Federal Register (91 Fed. Reg. 53741), the proposal would collapse roughly 995 existing size standards down to 338 consolidated categories, organized at the 4- and 5-digit NAICS code level. The SBA estimates that approximately 114,541 firms would gain "small" status under the new framework. Fewer than 200 would lose it. Those are not small numbers. The comment period closed September 21, then got extended 60 days to November 20, 2026, per Regulations.gov Docket SBA-2026-0199. A final rule is not expected until sometime in 2027, so there's a window right now to understand where you stand.

The timing is real. If your business is near a size threshold today, the rule change could open or close doors to capital and contracts within the next 12 to 18 months.

## Why the Current System Is Broken

The existing size standards were built industry by industry over decades. Some are based on annual revenue. Some use employee headcount. The thresholds vary wildly and were often set at a time when $10 million meant something different than it does now. Inflation alone has quietly pushed businesses past revenue caps that haven't been updated, knocking them out of "small" status even when their workforce and market position haven't changed.

That's one reason the SBA wants to shift the default measure. For most non-service industries, the proposal moves away from revenue thresholds toward employee counts. The logic: headcount doesn't spike because of a hot quarter or a price surge in your supply chain. Revenue does. An employee-based standard creates more stability for businesses trying to plan around their eligibility.

## What Actually Changes for Your Industry

The range of proposed threshold changes is significant. Some industries would see modest adjustments. Others would see dramatic rewrites. The computer systems design sector is the clearest example. Under the current rule, NAICS 5415 carries a $34 million revenue threshold. Under the proposed standard, that number jumps to $531 million, according to the SBA's own published figures. That's not a rounding error. That's a reclassification that would pull a large chunk of the mid-market tech services sector into small business eligibility overnight.

Here's a side-by-side look at how the structure of the rule itself changes:

| Feature | Current System | Proposed Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Number of size standards | ~995 | 338 |
| Primary measure (most industries) | Annual revenue | Employee headcount |
| Standard grouping level | 6-digit NAICS (narrow) | 4- and 5-digit NAICS (broader) |
| NAICS 5415 revenue threshold | $34 million | $531 million |
| Estimated firms gaining small status |, | ~114,541 |
| Estimated firms losing small status |, | Fewer than 200 |

The consolidation from narrow to broader NAICS codes is itself a significant structural shift. Right now, two companies in closely related sub-industries might face different thresholds. Under the proposal, they'd fall under the same standard. That could help businesses that were caught in an oddly narrow category with a low cap.

## What This Means for SBA Loans and Federal Contracts

Gaining "small" status isn't just symbolic. It's the entry point for SBA 7(a) loans, which cap at $5 million and are the most common SBA product, and 504 loans, which are structured for real estate and major equipment purchases and go up to $5.5 million for most borrowers. The Wiley Law analysis of the proposed rule noted that firms crossing into small status would immediately become eligible for these programs and for federal contracting set-asides that are reserved specifically for small businesses.

One honest caution on the loan side: rates aren't where they were two or three years ago. The Fed raised rates a quarter point in September 2026 and held steady at its October 2026 meeting. SBA 7(a) loans are variable-rate products tied to the prime rate, so borrowing costs remain elevated. Qualifying as small gets you in the door. It doesn't guarantee affordable debt. If you're newly eligible and considering an SBA loan, run the numbers carefully or work with a lender who can walk you through the actual cost of capital right now.

For federal contracting, the calculus is different. Set-asides don't carry an interest rate. If your firm can now compete for contracts it was locked out of before, that's direct revenue opportunity, not a financing cost question.

## How to Check Where You Stand Before the Final Rule

The final rule won't land until 2027, but that doesn't mean you should wait. Here's the practical move: find your 6-digit NAICS code, then look at how the SBA's proposal would consolidate it into the new 4- or 5-digit framework. The proposed rule and its supporting tables are publicly available through the SBA Office of Advocacy's August 20, 2026 release. Cross-reference your current revenue and headcount against both the existing threshold and the proposed one.

If you're currently above a revenue cap but under the proposed employee-count threshold, you may gain eligibility when the final rule publishes. If you're currently small under a revenue-based standard and the new framework switches your industry to headcount, verify that your headcount keeps you in. Most businesses will come out ahead. But "most" isn't the same as "all."

This is also a smart time to talk to a government contracts attorney or a CPA familiar with SBA programs. The Congressional Research Service flagged in its 2026 analysis that the rule's breadth raises real implementation questions, and the rulemaking process isn't finished. A professional who tracks this space can tell you what the transition period might look like and whether you should be positioning now.

## What To Watch Between Now and 2027

The comment period closes November 20, 2026. After that, the SBA works through the public feedback and drafts a final rule. Changes between the proposed and final versions are possible, particularly for industries where trade associations pushed back hard during the comment period. Watch the Federal Register and the SBA Office of Advocacy's site for the final publication date.

If your business is on the boundary, don't restructure or make major capital decisions based on the proposed rule alone. It's not final. But you can absolutely use the proposal as a planning framework, map your eligibility scenarios, and have conversations with your lender or contracting officer so you're not scrambling when the final rule drops.

The SBA is not in the business of making itself smaller. This proposal expands access, and significantly so. If your business has been sitting on the wrong side of an arbitrary threshold for years, that may be about to change.
