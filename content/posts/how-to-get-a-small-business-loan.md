## What Lenders Actually Look At (And What They Won't Tell You)

Banks and lenders aren't doing you a favor when they approve a loan. They're making a calculated bet that you'll pay them back, with interest. Understanding that changes how you prepare.

Most lenders evaluate small business loan applications using some version of the "Five Cs of Credit": character, capacity, capital, collateral, and conditions. You'll see this framework referenced by the [U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA)](https://www.sba.gov/), and it's genuinely useful shorthand for what's happening when someone reviews your file.

Here's what each one actually means in practice:

**Character** is your credit history, both personal and business. A FICO score below 650 will close a lot of doors. Not all of them, but most of the traditional ones. Some online lenders will work with scores in the 580 range, but expect higher interest rates and tighter terms.

**Capacity** is your ability to repay. Lenders look at your Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR), which compares your net operating income to your total debt obligations. A DSCR of 1.25 or higher is typically what traditional banks want to see. That means for every dollar you owe in loan payments, your business generates $1.25 in net income.

**Capital** is what you've personally put into the business. Lenders want skin in the game. If you're asking for $100,000 but have put almost nothing in yourself, that's a red flag.

**Collateral** is the asset backstop. Equipment, real estate, inventory. Not every loan requires it, but having it strengthens your application considerably.

**Conditions** refers to the purpose of the loan, the industry you're in, and the broader economic climate. A restaurant loan application in a tough economy gets more scrutiny than a manufacturing loan in a growth industry.

Know these five things cold before you walk into any meeting.

## The Main Types of Small Business Loans (And When to Use Each)

Not every loan is the right loan. Choosing the wrong product can cost you thousands in interest or leave you with terms your cash flow can't support.

**SBA Loans** are partially guaranteed by the federal government, which reduces risk for lenders and typically results in better terms for borrowers. The SBA 7(a) loan is the most common, available up to $5 million, and is used for working capital, equipment, real estate, and more. SBA 504 loans are structured specifically for major fixed assets like commercial real estate or heavy equipment. These loans take longer to close (often 60 to 90 days) but carry lower interest rates and longer repayment periods. They're worth the wait if you qualify.

**Traditional Bank Term Loans** are straightforward: you borrow a lump sum and repay it over a fixed term with interest. They're best for businesses with at least two years of operating history, solid revenue, and good credit. If you have all three, this can be your cheapest option.

**Business Lines of Credit** work more like a credit card. You get access to a set credit limit, draw from it as needed, and only pay interest on what you use. Great for managing cash flow gaps, covering seasonal inventory, or handling unexpected expenses. Not ideal for large one-time capital purchases.

**Equipment Financing** is tied to a specific piece of equipment. The equipment itself serves as collateral, which makes approval easier and rates competitive. If you're buying machinery, vehicles, or technology, this is usually the cleanest structure.

**Invoice Financing / Factoring** lets you borrow against your outstanding receivables. If you have $50,000 in unpaid invoices sitting there while you can't make payroll, this can be a lifeline. The cost is higher than traditional lending, but it's faster and doesn't always require great credit.

**Microloans** are small loans (typically under $50,000) offered through nonprofit lenders, community development financial institutions (CDFIs), and SBA intermediaries. These are excellent for startups or businesses that don't yet qualify for traditional financing.

## How to Prepare Your Application: A Step-by-Step Approach

Preparation is where most people lose before the race even starts. Here's what to get in order before you apply anywhere.

**Step 1: Pull your credit reports.**
Check both your personal credit (annualcreditreport.com) and your business credit (Dun & Bradstreet, Experian Business, Equifax Business). Dispute any errors. This alone has saved clients of mine from unnecessary rejections.

**Step 2: Get your financial documents organized.**
At minimum, you'll need the last two to three years of business tax returns, two to three years of personal tax returns, recent profit and loss statements (year-to-date), a current balance sheet, and recent bank statements (usually three to six months). Some lenders also want accounts receivable and payable aging reports.

**Step 3: Know your number before you ask.**
Don't walk in and say "I need some working capital." Know exactly how much you need and why. Build a simple projection showing how the loan funds will be used and how the resulting revenue or cost savings will service the debt. A one-page use-of-funds summary goes a long way.

**Step 4: Write a business plan or executive summary.**
For larger loans, especially SBA loans, you'll need a formal business plan. For smaller amounts, a tight two-page executive summary often suffices. It should cover your business model, market, competitive position, and financial projections. If you want a structured guide for this, Mike Michalowicz's *[Profit First](https://www.amazon.com/Profit-First-Transform-Cash-Eating-Money-Making/dp/073521414X?tag=YOURTAG-20)* isn't a business plan book per se, but it reframes how you think about your business finances in a way that makes lenders sit up straighter. *(Affiliate link: this site may earn a small commission on purchases.)*

**Step 5: Research lenders before you apply.**
Every hard inquiry on your credit can knock a few points off your score. Apply strategically. Look at community banks and credit unions first, then SBA-approved lenders, then online lenders if needed. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's small business resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) include guidance on understanding loan terms and knowing your rights as a borrower. Worth a read before you sign anything.

**Step 6: Apply and follow up.**
Once you submit, stay responsive. A lender asking for an additional document isn't a bad sign. Going silent for a week is. Treat it like a job interview where the follow-up matters.

## Comparing Your Loan Options

Here's a quick reference to help you match your situation to the right product:

| Loan Type | Best For | Typical Loan Amount | Speed | Credit Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SBA 7(a) | Most business purposes | Up to $5M | 30-90 days | 650+ |
| SBA 504 | Real estate, equipment | Up to $5.5M | 60-90 days | 680+ |
| Bank Term Loan | Established businesses | Varies widely | 2-6 weeks | 680+ |
| Business Line of Credit | Cash flow management | $10K-$500K | 1-4 weeks | 620+ |
| Equipment Financing | Specific equipment | Up to equipment value | 1-2 weeks | 600+ |
| Microloan | Startups, small needs | Up to $50K | 2-4 weeks | Flexible |
| Invoice Financing | B2B with slow-pay clients | Based on receivables | Days | Low emphasis |

These ranges are general. Your actual terms will depend on your specific profile, industry, lender, and loan purpose. A CPA or financial advisor can help you model the true cost of each option before you commit.

## When You Get Rejected (And What to Do Next)

Rejection stings. But it's not a verdict on your business. It's information.

Ask the lender specifically why you were declined. Many won't volunteer this, but you can ask. Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, commercial lenders are required to provide reasons for denial in certain circumstances. Understanding the "why" tells you exactly what to work on.

Common reasons for small business loan rejections include: insufficient time in business (less than two years), low personal credit score, weak cash flow, too much existing debt, inadequate collateral, or incomplete documentation.

I've seen clients get rejected in January and approved by June simply by paying down a personal credit card, correcting a credit report error, and cleaning up their bookkeeping. Six months of disciplined preparation can flip the result entirely.

If traditional lenders aren't an option right now, consider CDFIs (Community Development Financial Institutions), which exist specifically to serve underbanked businesses and often have more flexible criteria. The SBA's website has a CDFI locator tool. Revenue-based financing is another alternative worth exploring if your business has consistent monthly revenue but thin credit history.

One more thing: don't let the urgency of a cash need push you into predatory financing. Merchant cash advances (MCAs) can carry effective annual percentage rates north of 100%. They may be the only option in a genuine emergency, but they should not be a routine financing tool. Read everything carefully, and have a CPA or attorney review any agreement you're not certain about.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to get a small business loan?

It depends entirely on the loan type and lender. Online lenders can fund in as little as 24 to 72 hours for smaller amounts. Traditional bank loans typically take two to six weeks. SBA loans can take 30 to 90 days because of the government guarantee process. If speed is your primary constraint, an online lender or line of credit will serve you better than an SBA loan.

### Do I need collateral to get a small business loan?

Not always. Some unsecured business loans and lines of credit don't require specific collateral, though many still ask for a personal guarantee, which means you're personally liable if the business defaults. SBA loans under $25,000 typically don't require collateral. Above that threshold, lenders will expect you to pledge available business or personal assets.

### What credit score do I need for a small business loan?

For traditional bank loans and SBA loans, aim for a personal credit score of at least 650, ideally 680 or higher. Online lenders often work with scores as low as 550 to 600, but at considerably higher rates. Your business credit score matters too, especially for larger loans or if you're trying to separate personal liability from your business finances.

### Can a startup get a small business loan?

Yes, but your options are narrower. Most traditional lenders want to see at least two years of operating history. Startups often do better with SBA microloans, CDFI financing, equipment financing (if buying specific assets), or business credit cards to start building credit history. Having a strong personal credit score and putting up collateral improves your odds considerably.

### Should I use a loan broker to find financing?

A broker can save time by shopping your application across multiple lenders, which is genuinely useful if you're not sure where to start. The downside is that brokers are paid by lenders (or charge you a fee), which creates an incentive to place you with whoever pays best, not necessarily whoever's right for you. If you use one, ask upfront how they're compensated and get competing quotes on your own as a check.

Getting a small business loan is not a mysterious process reserved for businesses that already have everything figured out. It's a preparation game. Know your numbers, understand what lenders are evaluating, match the right loan type to your actual need, and show up with documentation that makes their job easy. The businesses that get funded aren't always the strongest ones. They're the most prepared ones. That part is squarely in your control.
