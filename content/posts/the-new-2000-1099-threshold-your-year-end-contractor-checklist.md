December 31 is a few weeks away, and if you've been paying independent contractors in 2026, you have a real deadline bearing down on you. The One [Big Beautiful Bill](/how-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-changes-your-2026-llc-tax-strategy/) Act, signed in 2025, raised the 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC reporting threshold from $600 to $2,000, effective for payments made this calendar year. That's the first change to this threshold since the 1950s. The forms covering those payments are due in January 2027, which means your window to audit every vendor total and collect missing W-9s is closing fast.

Here's where owners are getting tripped up. The higher threshold sounds like relief, and in some ways it is. You won't have to file a 1099-NEC for the freelance photographer you paid $800 for one project. But the rule is not as simple as "ignore everyone under $2,000," and the attorney you paid $1,500 for contract review is still fully reportable. NFIB's October 2026 survey found that 21% of [small business owners](/sba-loans-now-require-100-us-citizenship-what-small-business-owners-mu/) now cite taxes as their single biggest problem, the highest share since May 2021. Year-end 1099 compliance is exactly the kind of thing that creates that anxiety, especially when the rules just changed.

Before you assume you've got fewer forms to file, run the actual numbers. The traps are specific, and they're sitting in your [accounting software](/accounting-software-comparison/) right now.

## The Aggregation Trap Most Owners Miss

The $2,000 threshold applies to the total you paid a single contractor across the entire calendar year, not per invoice or per project. If you paid a bookkeeper $400 in March, $600 in June, $500 in September, and $600 in December, that's $2,100 total. A 1099-NEC is required, even though no single payment crossed $2,000. This is not new law. It's how aggregation has always worked. What's new is that owners who were previously filing for anyone over $600 now have to think more carefully about who lands just above and just below the new line.

Pull every contractor from your accounting system right now and sort by total paid in 2026. Don't sort by payment count or last invoice amount. Total paid, year to date. That single report will tell you who needs a form.

## The Attorney and Royalty Exceptions Are Not Small Print

This is the most common compliance mistake owners make when thresholds change. Owners read the headline number, assume it applies across the board, and miss the carve-outs.

Gross proceeds paid to attorneys are still reportable at $600 or more. That applies even when the payment is not for the attorney's services specifically, such as a settlement payment that passes through their account. The $2,000 threshold does not apply here. If you retained outside counsel this year and payments exceeded $600 total, you need a 1099-MISC, not a pass.

Royalties carry their own threshold: $10 or more. That hasn't moved. If your business pays royalties to a licensor or content creator under a licensing arrangement, those get reported at a level that is basically "any meaningful amount."

Here's a quick-reference breakdown of the thresholds that matter for your 2026 filings:

| Payment Type | Form | 2026 Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor / freelancer services | 1099-NEC | $2,000 |
| Rents, prizes, other income | 1099-MISC | $2,000 |
| Gross proceeds paid to attorneys | 1099-MISC | $600 |
| Royalties | 1099-MISC | $10 |
| Payment apps / platforms (1099-K) | 1099-K | $20,000 and 200 transactions |

As AAFCPAs noted in their November 2026 guidance, year-end preparation remains familiar even when thresholds shift. The form types, the aggregation rules, and the attorney exception all work the same way they always have. What changed is the number on the NEC and MISC lines for general contractor payments.

## The 1099-K Situation Is Separate but Relevant

If your business receives payments through platforms like PayPal, Venmo for Business, or Square, the 1099-K threshold is now $20,000 and 200 transactions, restored to that level by the OBBBA after years of the IRS delaying a planned drop to $600. This matters for your recordkeeping in two directions.

As a payer, the 1099-K rules don't change your obligation to file 1099-NECs for contractors you pay directly. Those are separate tracks. But if you're also a recipient, as many small business owners are when clients pay through apps, you now have more clarity on when platforms will report your income to the IRS. The chaotic $600 proposal that kept getting delayed is off the table for now.

## W-9s and Missing Information: Fix It Before January

You cannot file a 1099 without a taxpayer identification number. If you've added new contractors this year and skipped collecting a W-9, you've got two to three weeks to fix that before the holiday slowdown makes it nearly impossible to track people down. Send the request now, in writing, and keep a record that you asked. If a contractor refuses to provide their TIN, backup withholding rules apply and you're looking at a 24% withholding obligation on future payments.

Also confirm whether each contractor is an individual, a sole proprietor, or an LLC. Single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietors require a 1099-NEC. LLCs taxed as S-corps or C-corps generally do not, with the attorney exception still applying. The W-9 form tells you which box applies. If you don't have a current W-9 on file, you're guessing, and guessing on this creates exposure.

Heaven CPA's 2026 overview makes the point clearly: the new threshold doesn't reduce your recordkeeping burden as much as people expect, because you still need to track every contractor's total to know whether you've crossed $2,000. The administrative work mostly stays the same.

## What to Do Right Now, Before Year-End

Run your contractor payment report sorted by total 2026 payments. Flag everyone above $2,000, then separately flag all attorney payments above $600 and any royalty payments above $10. For anyone in those buckets, confirm you have a valid W-9 with a matching name and TIN. If anything is missing, contact those contractors before December 31. After the new year, you're filing in January and chasing W-9s simultaneously, which is a bad place to be.

Also check whether any of your vendors who you've been treating as corporations are actually sole proprietors or single-member LLCs. That mistake is surprisingly common and can result in missing 1099s. Your accountant or CPA can help you review the classification if you're uncertain. LLC O CPA's 2026 filing guide notes that the form type and recipient classification questions haven't changed, even though the dollar threshold has.

Starting in 2027, the $2,000 threshold will adjust for inflation each year, so 2026 is the last time this will be a clean, fixed number. The IRS has not yet released how that adjustment will be calculated or announced, so don't assume the number holds for next year.

The compliance window here is short, the rules have real exceptions, and the January filing deadline doesn't move. Get your contractor list reconciled now, while you can still reach people and correct your records cleanly. If you have any doubt about your specific situation, work with your CPA before the end of the year. This is one of those cases where a one-hour review can prevent a filing penalty and an amended form headache in February.
