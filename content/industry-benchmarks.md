  - q: "What is gross margin vs. net margin?"
    a: "Gross margin is revenue minus direct costs (cost of goods sold, direct labor) divided by revenue. It shows how much you keep after delivering your product or service before overhead. Net margin is what remains after ALL expenses including rent, payroll, marketing, insurance, debt service, and taxes. For benchmarking, gross margin tells you if your pricing model is viable; net margin tells you if the whole business model works."

Before launching or scaling a small business, one of the most valuable exercises you can do is benchmark your expected financials against real industry data. What net margin is realistic in your sector? How much revenue should each employee generate? What does it typically cost to get off the ground? These benchmarks will not tell you exactly what your business will achieve — geography, competition, operator quality, and countless other factors matter — but they give you a grounded starting point for your business plan and loan application.

## Reading the Profit Health Tiers

The table uses three profit health tiers to quickly flag which business models generate the most margin:

**Healthy (15%+ net margin potential):** These businesses have structural advantages — low overhead relative to revenue, recurring clients, high skill premiums, or near-zero marginal costs. Consulting, SaaS, professional services, and trades like plumbing and HVAC fall here. They are not necessarily easy businesses, but the economic model supports reinvestment and wealth-building.

**Moderate (5–15% net margin):** These businesses are viable but require more operational discipline. Restaurants, retail, salons, and fitness studios fall here. Margins are squeezable and often require volume, strong retention, or ancillary revenue streams to sustain. Cash flow management is more critical.

**Lean (under 5% net margin):** These businesses face structural margin compression from regulation, labor intensity, or price competition. Childcare, for example, is constrained by mandated staff-to-child ratios that limit revenue per employee. Lean-margin businesses can still support the owner's income goals, but they leave little buffer for downturns.

## Using Benchmarks in Your Business Plan

When building a financial model for an SBA loan application or investor presentation, benchmark-anchored projections carry more credibility than bottom-up guesses. If you project a 35% net margin for a restaurant, expect pushback — industry data says 3–9% is the norm. If you project 4% for a solo consulting practice, you may be leaving money on the table and signaling a pricing problem.

The most useful benchmark variables to anchor are: **gross margin** (if your model shows lower gross margin than industry average, you have a pricing or cost problem); **revenue per employee** (tells you how many people you will need at a target revenue); and **startup costs** (helps you size your SBA loan request appropriately and show the lender you have done your research).
